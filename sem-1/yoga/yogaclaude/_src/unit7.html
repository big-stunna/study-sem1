UNIT VII

Relaxation, Concentration & Meditation

Stress from studies, exams, competition, responsibilities and too much screen time can affect sleep, attention, memory and emotional well-being. Yoga gives simple tools: Shavasana, breath awareness, body scan, Om meditation, mindfulness and concentration exercises. Relaxation releases physical and mental tension · concentration focuses the mind on one object or activity · meditation builds deeper awareness and calmness. Regular practice supports emotional balance, self-awareness, attention and well-being.

1. Shavasana (Corpse Pose)

In one line: Shava = corpse. Body still and relaxed, like lying down peacefully — but with gentle awareness. Looks simple; needs conscious relaxation of body and mind.
Procedure
  1. Lie flat on your back on a mat or comfortable firm surface.
  2. Legs slightly apart, feet fall outward.
  3. Arms beside body, a little away from torso, palms up.
  4. Close eyes gently if comfortable.
  5. Let the whole body rest, no unnecessary movement.
  6. Watch natural breathing; don't control it.
  7. Relax feet, legs, abdomen, shoulders, arms, neck, face.
  8. Let thoughts come and go without getting involved.
  9. Stay about 5–10 min (as comfortable).
  10. To finish: deepen breath gently, move fingers and toes, turn to one side, sit up slowly.
Benefits
  • Reduces physical tension; relaxation
  • Calmness; may reduce stress
  • Notice needless muscle tension
  • Rest after exercise or yoga
  • Awareness of breath and sensations
  • May support better sleep (as part of a relaxing routine)
  • Patience; ability to stay still
Precautions
  • Quiet, comfortable, ventilated place
  • Pillow under knees or head if needed
  • Lying on the back uncomfortable → supported side-lying
  • Don't fall asleep on purpose — aim is conscious relaxation

Educational importance: rest is essential for health; teaches the difference between sleeping and consciously relaxing while staying aware.

2. Yogic Relaxation

Meaning: consciously releasing physical, mental and emotional tension through awareness, breathing and stillness. Unlike ordinary rest (where you may stay distracted by thoughts), you deliberately bring attention to body and mind. Can be done lying, sitting or in another suitable posture.

5 componentsMeaning
PhysicalRelease needless muscle tension
MentalLet the mind settle; less engagement with thoughts, worries, distractions
EmotionalRecognise emotions without reacting at once
BreathWatch natural breathing; let it become comfortable and steady
SensoryLess attention to outside distractions; aware of inner sensations
Procedure (about 5–15 min): quiet place, sit or lie comfortably → eyes closed if comfortable → a few gentle natural breaths → attention to feet, release tension → move attention up: legs, hips, abdomen, chest, shoulders, arms, neck, face → notice tight muscles and let them soften → watch breathing without forcing deeper/slower → thoughts arise: acknowledge, return to relaxation → finish slowly by moving gently and opening eyes.

Benefits: recovery from daily demands · less perceived stress and restlessness · awareness of tension and emotions · self-control and patience · readiness for study or work · general well-being with sleep, activity and healthy habits. For students: long sitting, studying and screens cause muscle tension and mental fatigue — yogic relaxation is a pause to release tension and return with more comfort and awareness.

3. Breath Awareness

Meaning: observing natural breathing without changing its speed, depth or rhythm. Builds present-moment awareness and helps you notice changes linked to tension, excitement or calm. One of the simplest introductory practices in yoga and meditation.

Procedure (3–10 min): sit on chair/floor with spine naturally upright (or lie down) → relax shoulders, arms, face → eyes closed or gaze lowered → attention to the breath → feel air entering and leaving the nostrils → feel chest or abdomen move → notice natural length and rhythm of each inhale and exhale → no forced deep breaths, no holding → mind wanders: bring it back to the next breath.

Benefits: present-moment awareness · notice breathing changes with emotion · may reduce stress and restlessness · patience and sustained attention · a pause before reacting · foundation for advanced meditation.

Example: nervous before an exam and breathing fast? Don't react — watch the breath for a minute or two to settle. Precautions: no retention, forceful or very deep breathing; dizziness/breathlessness/discomfort → normal breathing, stop if it continues.

4. Body Scan & Body Awareness

Body scan: a mindfulness technique that moves attention systematically through the body, noticing warmth, coolness, pressure, tingling, tension, relaxation, floor contact — with curiosity and acceptance, not judging or fixing every sensation. Body awareness: the ability to recognise sensations, posture, movement, muscle tension and other physical signals (e.g. tight shoulders after long sitting; tense jaw when anxious).

Procedure (5–15 min; shorter version for class breaks): lie on a mat or sit relaxed → natural breathing → toes and feet → notice sensations without judging → ankles, calves, knees, thighs → hips, abdomen, chest, back → hands, fingers, arms, shoulders → neck, jaw, cheeks, eyes, forehead → feel the body as a whole → return to surroundings.

Example: a scan shows raised, tense shoulders → relax them and adjust sitting.

5. Om Meditation

Meaning: attention on the sound, vibration or silent repetition of the sacred syllable Om (Aum). In Indian traditions Om is a sacred sound linked with consciousness and the deeper nature of existence; it steadies attention and brings inner stillness. Can be chanted aloud, repeated softly, or recalled mentally in silence.

Procedure (5–10 rounds, or a few minutes): sit comfortably, back naturally upright → shoulders relaxed, hands on thighs/knees → eyes closed if comfortable → 2–3 natural breaths → inhale comfortably → on the exhale chant “Om” slowly and gently (optional) → let the sound end naturally, no voice strain → notice sound and vibrations → pause and notice the silence after each repetition → or repeat Om silently in the mind → finish sitting quietly a little before opening eyes.

Benefits: a single point of focus · relaxation, calmness · awareness of sound and breath · regular meditation and self-reflection · prepares for tasks needing attention · introduces an important traditional yoga practice. Precautions: comfortable volume and pitch · don't force the voice or stretch the exhale · prefer not to chant → silent repetition or breath awareness · respect individual beliefs and cultural preferences in class. Importance: shows how sound, repetition, silence and steady awareness work in meditation.

6. Basic Mindfulness Practice

Mindfulness = paying attention to the present moment on purpose, with openness, curiosity and non-judgment — noticing body and mind without calling an experience good or bad. It does not mean stopping all thoughts or an empty mind; you notice thoughts, feelings and sensations and gently return to the present activity.

6 elements: present-moment awareness · non-judgment · acceptance · patience · awareness of thoughts and emotions (without automatically acting) · intentional attention (choose where it goes).

Procedure (3–5 min at first): sit comfortably in a quiet place → settle, natural breath → choose one thing: breath, sounds around you, or feet on the floor → pay attention to it as it happens → notice when the mind goes to past, future or other topics → acknowledge without criticism → gently return → slowly lengthen the time → at the end notice surroundings, resume activity mindfully.
Mindfulness in daily life
Eating — taste, smell, texture, look; eat slowlyWalking — leg movement, feet on ground, surroundings
Listening — listen without planning your replyStudying — stay with the lesson; no notification-checking or task-switching
Breathing — watch the breath a few moments before reacting to stress

Benefits: present-moment awareness · attention and emotional regulation · thoughtful responses instead of reactions · notice distraction and return to work · patience, self-awareness, careful listening · may reduce perceived stress. Important: it won't stop you ever feeling angry, worried or distracted — it teaches you to recognise these and respond with more awareness.

7. Concentration Practice

Concentration = directing attention to one object, thought, activity or task for a period while reducing needless distraction. In yoga it relates to Dharana (fixing the mind on a chosen point or object). Learning needs it: attend to information, understand, remember, finish tasks accurately.

5 types of concentration practice
1 Breath — natural movement of breathing2 Visual — look steadily at a simple object, e.g. a dot on paper
3 Sound — listen to a continuous or repeated sound4 Mental repetition — repeat a word/phrase silently
5 Single-task — one activity at a time, e.g. read one paragraph or solve one maths problem
Simple exercise (2–5 min): sit comfortably in a quiet place → pick an object (dot on paper) or the natural breath → visual object at comfortable viewing distance → direct attention to it → observe carefully, don't hunt for distractions → wandering mind: recognise it → gently return → lengthen the time gradually → end and notice how you feel.
Dot example: small black dot on white paper at eye level; look 1–2 min; each time the mind wanders, bring it back. Goal: not to stare hard or block every thought, but to practise noticing distraction and returning.
Benefits: trains sustained attention · supports academic work and finishing tasks · patience, mental discipline · notice common distractions · careful observation and listening · easier return to a task after interruption.
Factors affecting concentration: lack of sleep · noise and distractions · stress and emotional worries · hunger, thirst, physical discomfort · too much multitasking and digital notifications · low interest or unclear goals.

Tips: clear goal before a task · comfortable, low-distraction place · breaks in long sessions · devices away when not needed · regular sleep · short concentration exercises regularly.

8. Basic Meditation Technique

Meditation = training attention and awareness for a more stable, clear and balanced relationship with thoughts, feelings and experience. In yoga it relates to Dhyana (a sustained flow of attention toward a chosen focus). Methods: watch the breath, repeat a mantra, attend to body sensations, or rest in open awareness. The mind need not become blank — thoughts come, and you notice them without being absorbed.

  1. Place — quiet and comfortable, few interruptions.
  2. Posture — chair or floor, spine naturally upright, not stiff.
  3. Relax — soften shoulders, hands, jaw, face.
  4. Settle attention — eyes closed or gaze lowered, if comfortable.
  5. Choose a focus — natural breath, a mantra, or a body sensation.
  6. Observe — pay attention to the focus; don't force anything.
  7. Recognise wandering — notice gently when the mind goes to memories, plans, worries.
  8. Return — bring attention back, no self-blame.
  9. Continue — start with 3–5 min, lengthen if comfortable.
  10. End slowly — notice body and surroundings before resuming activity.

Benefits: may reduce perceived stress, calm · awareness of thoughts, feelings, sensations · patience and non-reactivity · attention and emotional regulation · self-reflection · general psychological well-being with consistent practice.

9. Relaxation Through Controlled Breathing

Controlled breathing = gently, comfortably adjusting the pace or pattern of breathing. Linked to pranayama (which includes more techniques than simple slow breathing). Helpful when breathing is rapid or irregular from short-term stress.

Example: nervous before a presentation → a few gentle unforced breaths, smooth exhale.

10. Mental Calmness & Concentration — why they matter

Mental calmness = relative emotional steadiness; thinking and responding without being overwhelmed by stress, fear, anger or worry. It does not mean no emotions — you can still feel excitement, sadness, anxiety or frustration but recognise them and respond thoughtfully.
  1. Manage stress — respond better to academic/personal pressure
  2. Emotional regulation — recognise emotions before reacting
  3. Clear thinking — easier to organise thoughts, find solutions
  4. Healthy relationships — listen patiently, respond thoughtfully
  5. Self-awareness — know responses, needs, limits
  6. General well-being — balance of study, rest, personal life
Concentration = directing attention to a task or experience and holding it for a suitable period. Calmness and concentration support each other: overwhelmed → hard to focus; constantly interrupted → frustrated and tired.
  1. Learning efficiency — engage with lessons
  2. Memory — attending when information is given
  3. Completing tasks — sustained effort
  4. Fewer mistakes — notice details, follow instructions
  5. Discipline — routines for directing attention
  6. Problem-solving — examine information, consider approaches
How they connect (related, not identical): Relaxation reduces needless physical and mental tension · Mental calmness = emotional steadiness, less reactive · Concentration points attention at a task or object · Meditation trains awareness and attention systematically.
Example: Shavasana to release tension → breath awareness to settle attention → concentrate on a textbook chapter.

Everyday use: before studying — 2 min comfortable breathing · before an exam — watch the breath briefly, unforced · long study session — pause, notice and relax tense shoulders · in class — return attention whenever the mind wanders · before reacting to something upsetting — recognise the emotion, choose a thoughtful reply · end of day — brief body scan for tension.

Supporting habits: regular sleep · balanced meals and enough water · regular physical activity · reasonably organised study space · breaks in long sessions · fewer digital distractions · healthy communication · relaxation/meditation regularly, without expecting instant results.
