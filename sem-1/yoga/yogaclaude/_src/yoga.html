UNIT I

Foundation of Yoga

3 hours

1. Meaning, Definition & Objectives

In one line: Yoga comes from the Sanskrit root Yuj = “to join / unite / yoke” — joining body, mind, breath and awareness.
  • Holistic: it looks at the whole person, not just exercise.
  • General definition: a system of postures, breathing, concentration, meditation and relaxation for physical and mental well-being.
  • Patanjali: “Yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ” — controlling the ups and downs (fluctuations) of the mind.
  • Practical definition: a way to build health, calmness, self-awareness, discipline and a balanced life.

9 objectives: (1) physical health & fitness (2) flexibility, balance, coordination, body awareness (3) comfortable, controlled breathing (4) concentration (5) manage everyday stress (6) relaxation & emotional awareness (7) patience, discipline, self-control (8) healthy daily habits (9) self-awareness & a balanced approach to life.

3. Importance in Modern Life

Modern life = long sitting, screens, irregular routines, pressure. Yoga adds movement, breath awareness and relaxation.

  • Physical: flexibility, balance, posture, mobility, endurance; awareness of limits.
  • Mental: calmer mind, awareness of thoughts and emotions.
  • Stress: a pause to release tension and shift attention.
  • Concentration: practise returning attention when distracted.
  • Healthy habits: regular movement, self-discipline; works with sleep and nutrition.
  • Education: posture awareness while studying, pre-exam relaxation, patience, regular habits.
Limit: yoga is not a substitute for medical treatment or all other exercise — use it inside a balanced lifestyle.

5. Yoga & Mental Well-Being

Mental well-being = managing daily demands, understanding emotions, keeping relationships, deciding well. Yoga mixes movement, breath, attention and relaxation.

  • Less mental tension: stress shows as tight muscles, fast breathing, repeating thoughts — gentle yoga may settle it.
  • Emotional awareness: notice emotions without reacting at once (e.g. exam nervousness → brief relaxation).
  • Self-awareness: observe sensations, breath, attention, comfort.
  • Mood: some feel better; may help some anxiety/depression symptoms but results vary — not a replacement for professional care.
  • Patience & self-control: steady practice beats competing or forcing poses.
  • Students: routines of movement, relaxation, awareness; plus sleep, activity, social support, help when needed.

6. Yoga & Stress Management

Stress = body-and-mind response to demands or perceived threats. Some stress motivates; too much or too long disturbs sleep, concentration, mood. Student sources: exams, assignments, deadlines, family expectations, social pressure, uncertain future.

6 ways yoga helps: ① breathing awareness (less rapid breathing) ② muscle relaxation ③ attention regulation (away from worries) ④ body awareness (raised shoulders, tense jaw) ⑤ regular routine = self-care time ⑥ relaxation/rest after effort.

7. Yoga & Concentration

Concentration = directing attention to one task/object/thought while limiting distractions (needed for reading, listening, problem-solving). In yoga you focus on alignment and balance in an asana, or on the breath; when the mind wanders you gently bring it back — that repetition is the training.

Helpful practices: breath awareness, single-pointed attention, basic meditation, balance postures, body awareness, mindful movement with breath.

Breath counting (2–3 min): sit, spine naturally upright → shoulders and face relaxed → breathe naturally → count each full breath 1 to 5, then restart at 1 → if distracted, return to 1 without self-criticism.
Outcome: practise directing attention, noticing distraction, returning focus. Also helped by sleep, exercise, less screen time.

8. Yoga & Flexibility

Flexibility = ability of a joint (or joints) to move through an appropriate range. Depends on joint structure, tissue properties, age, activity level, past injuries. It supports bending, reaching, turning, sitting, walking — and is not the same as doing extreme poses. Yoga uses controlled postures that slowly take muscles through a comfortable range; improvement varies by person.

Examples: Tadasana (alignment, balance) · seated side stretch · Cat–Cow (gentle spine movement, hands and knees) · standing side bend.

7 principles: warm up first · move slowly, no jerks · stretch to a mild sensation only · breathe normally · never force a joint · practise regularly, not extremely once · modify for age, ability, health.

Stop on sharp pain, tingling, numbness or joint pain — these are not good stretch feelings.

10. Basic Principles of Yoga Practice

  1. Individual ability — adapt poses to you, not to others.
  2. Comfortable alignment — spine in natural position, no forced extreme joint ranges.
  3. Movement + breath — usually inhale while lengthening/opening, exhale while folding/relaxing (a general convention, not a rigid rule).
  4. No unnecessary force — no competitive stretching or jerks; progress gradually.
  5. Awareness — alignment, breath, tension, discomfort signs.
  6. Relaxed mind — aim to learn, be comfortable, move in control, not look perfect.
  7. Progress gradually — simple poses, short holds first.
  8. Systematic sequence — preparation → warm-up → main practice → breathing → relaxation.
  9. Suitable place — clean, ventilated, well-lit, obstacle-free, non-slip mat.
  10. Qualified instructor — correct alignment, modifications, safety; medical cases need individual advice.

11. Regularity & Discipline

Regularity = practising consistently over time. Discipline = following a suitable routine, respecting safety rules, practising with patience and commitment. Occasional practice gives temporary relaxation; lasting gains in flexibility, balance, strength need consistency.

Discipline gives: consistency & patience · attention to technique · following a plan · respect for limits · responsibility for health · no rushing into advanced practice.

Beginner routine: ~10–15 min sessions, increase slowly. A week = gentle yoga, joint mobility, breathing awareness, suitable asanas, relaxation, plus rest days/lighter sessions. Quality over quantity — a few poses done well beat many done wrong; never raise time/intensity at the cost of comfort or safety.

12. Role of Breathing

Breathing awareness = noticing natural inhalation and exhalation without trying to change it (watch abdomen, ribs, chest). Phases: inhalation (air in) · exhalation (air out) · natural pause. Beginners should not deliberately lengthen pauses or hold the breath.

Why it matters: ① supports relaxation ② improves awareness ③ coordinates movement ④ supports concentration ⑤ shows tension/effort/discomfort.

Abdominal breathing awareness (2–3 min): lie on back, knees bent, feet on floor (or sit supported) → one hand on abdomen → relax shoulders and face → breathe naturally, feel abdomen move → don't push it out or force the breath → return attention to surroundings before getting up.
Benefits: breathing awareness, a focus for attention, may support relaxation.

Basic yogic breathing types: natural breath awareness · diaphragmatic breathing · Nadi Shodhana (alternate-nostril) · Bhramari (gentle humming on exhale). Beginners start with natural and comfortable diaphragmatic breathing; specialised techniques only with supervision.

Precautions: never force breath · avoid long retention · no rapid/very deep breathing to dizziness · stop for dizziness, chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, distress · medical conditions → get professional guidance first.

13. Warm-Up & Preparation

Warm-up = gentle activity before the main session. 6 objectives: gradually increase movement & muscle activity · mobilise joints comfortably · prepare muscles · improve alignment awareness · find stiffness/discomfort early · set a calm, attentive approach.

Components: general preparation (stand/sit, natural breath) · joint mobility (neck, shoulders, wrists, hips, knees, ankles) · dynamic movements (shoulder rolls, arm raises, gentle spinal movement) · breathing awareness.

14. General Precautions

Yoga suits most people when adapted, but it is not risk-free; safety depends on health, practice type, instructor, and pose suitability.

WhenDo
BeforeClean, stable, non-slip surface · comfortable clothes · clear objects away · warm up · tell instructor about injuries/limits/health concerns · don't start a hard session when acutely unwell
DuringMove slowly with control · no jerks or over-stretching · don't compete · breathe comfortably, no needless breath-holding · use chair, wall, cushion, block · stop for sharp pain, numbness, tingling, unusual discomfort · don't continue just because others can
AfterRelax · rise gradually from lying · notice how you feel · drink water · avoid rushing into demanding activity if light-headed

Need modified practice and professional guidance: recent surgery, acute injuries, severe balance problems, certain heart conditions, uncontrolled blood pressure, glaucoma, pregnancy concerns. Not every posture suits every person.

Stop if: sharp/worsening pain · dizziness or faintness · chest pain or pressure · unusual/severe shortness of breath · new numbness or tingling · sudden weakness or loss of balance. Chest pain, fainting or severe breathing trouble = urgent medical attention.

15. Contraindications

Contraindication = a condition where a posture/technique should be avoided because it may be risky. It may apply to one practice, not to all yoga (e.g. cannot kneel → still do seated or standing work).

ConditionPrecaution / modification
Neck pain/injuryAvoid forceful neck movements and postures that load the unsupported neck
Lower-back pain/injuryAvoid painful deep forward bends, forceful twists, movements that aggravate symptoms
Knee pain/injuryAvoid painful kneeling or deep knee bends; use supports or alternatives
Wrist painModify weight-bearing poses or use a non-weight-bearing alternative
High blood pressureAvoid demanding inversions or strenuous breath-holding unless medically cleared and supervised
Glaucoma / certain eye conditionsSeek specialist advice on head-down/inversion postures
PregnancyPregnancy-appropriate modifications; guidance from qualified instructor and healthcare professional
Recent surgeryMedical clearance before resuming physical practice
Dizziness / balance problemsSupported postures; avoid unsupported balance challenges
Breathing / cardiovascular disordersSuitable intensity and breathing practices, with professional guidance

These are general examples, not a medical assessment — diagnosis, severity, recovery stage and the doctor's advice all matter.

Contraindication vs modification: contraindication = don't do that posture/technique in those circumstances; modification = adapt it to be easier or more suitable (hold a wall/chair for balance; seated option for knee pain). Individualisation: adapt yoga to the person, not the person to the pose. Teachers give alternatives, respect feedback, and don't diagnose or treat beyond their competence.

16. Relaxation After Practice

Relaxation = reducing unnecessary muscle tension and letting body and mind rest (lying, sitting, watching breath, or relaxing body parts one by one).

Why: ① reduces leftover tension (shoulders, jaw, hands) ② encourages calmness ③ develops body awareness ④ gives a gradual end to the session ⑤ practises mindful attention to the present.

Shavasana (Corpse Pose) — about 3–5 min or more: lie on back → legs rest apart naturally → arms beside body, palms up (or comfortable) → cushion for head/knees if needed → eyes closed or softly open → watch natural breath → relax face, shoulders, arms, abdomen, legs, feet → stay still → deepen awareness of surroundings before moving fingers and toes → roll to one side, sit up slowly.

Alternatives (back pain, pregnancy, breathing difficulty, discomfort): supported side-lying, reclined, or chair. Nobody should be forced to close eyes, lie a certain way, or stay still if it distresses them.

19. Basic Breathing Awareness

Observing natural breathing without forcing, speeding up or controlling it — the simplest link between breath, attention and relaxation. Objectives: awareness of inhalation/exhalation · comfortable breathing · attention to sensations · a basic way to settle attention · preparation for structured breathing later.

Seated breathing awareness (2–3 min): sit cross-legged on mat, on a cushion, or on a chair with feet supported → spine naturally upright → hands on thighs → relax shoulders, jaw, face → eyes closed or softly open → watch breath enter and leave → notice abdomen/chest/ribs moving → don't take deliberately deeper breaths → if mind wanders, return to the breath → at the end notice surroundings and resume activity gradually.

Learning outcomes: explain breathing awareness · identify inhalation/exhalation · keep a comfortable sitting posture · observe natural breath without forcing · see why attention and relaxation matter · recognise signs to stop.

Teacher: speak slowly, simple instructions; no identical breathing pattern for all (it varies). If a student has dizziness, breathlessness, panic or discomfort → stop focusing on the breath, breathe normally, rest.

20. Correct Sitting & Standing Posture

Posture = how the body is positioned when sitting, standing, moving. Aim: stable, comfortable alignment without strain. No single rigid posture suits everyone — the goal is comfortable alignment, not a perfectly straight body at all times.

Sitting (mat, cushion or chair)
  1. Legs comfortable if cross-legged; don't force knees to floor
  2. Pelvis stable
  3. Spine keeps natural curves
  4. Head balanced over shoulders
  5. Shoulders relaxed, not pulled back
  6. Hands on thighs or knees
  7. Jaw and face relaxed
  8. Breathe naturally
Benefits: spinal-alignment awareness; stable base for breathing/meditation; less needless tension; finds a comfortable position for study. Mistakes: slumping · raised shoulders · exaggerated back arch · rigid body · cross-legged despite knee/hip pain. Modification: chair, cushion or other supported position.
Standing (feet hip-width or comfortable)
  1. Weight spread over both feet
  2. Knees relaxed, not locked
  3. Pelvis and spine natural
  4. Chest open without pushing forward
  5. Shoulders rest naturally
  6. Head balanced over trunk
  7. Look forward at a comfortable level
  8. Breathe normally, stay relaxed
This is the basic alignment of Tadasana (Mountain Pose).

UNIT II

Standing & Balancing Asanas

5 hours

Done mainly standing. They teach body alignment, weight distribution, breath coordination and stability. Balancing asanas add awareness of the body's position in space. Benefits: fitness, balance, coordination, flexibility, muscular endurance, body awareness, patience, concentration, confidence.

10 asanas: Tadasana · Vrikshasana · Utkatasana · Trikonasana · Konasana · Parshvakonasana · Ardha Chandrasana · Hastapadasana · Padahastasana · Garudasana

1
TadasanaMountain Pose

Tada = mountain. The base standing pose: teaches steady standing, weight distribution and natural spine alignment. Many other standing poses start from or pass through it.

Start: Stand upright on a level, non-slip surface · feet together or slightly apart · weight even on both feet · knees relaxed (not locked) · arms beside body · spine natural, head balanced over trunk · look straight ahead, breathe normally.

  1. Make a stable base through both feet.
  2. Feel the heels, base of big toes and base of little toes on the floor.
  3. Lengthen gently through the crown of the head; neck not stiff.
  4. Shoulders relax away from ears.
  5. Chest comfortably open — don't push ribs forward.
  6. Pelvis and lower back in natural position.
  7. Arms relaxed at sides, or raised overhead in an instructed variation.
  8. Hold while watching natural breath.
  9. To finish, relax and return to ordinary standing if you did a variation.
Breathing: Natural while setting up · Inhale as you lengthen the spine / raise arms · Exhale naturally, relaxing shoulders · Smooth breathing while holdingHold: Beginners 15–30 s · repeat 2–3 times · increase gradually
Benefits
  • Awareness of standing alignment
  • Balanced weight distribution
  • Better postural control
  • Awareness of feet, knees, pelvis, spine
  • Stable start for other standing asanas
  • Concentration and calmness through stillness
Precautions
  • Balance difficulty → feet slightly apart
  • Don't lock the knees
  • Don't exaggerate the lower-back arch
  • Dizzy → use support, stop if it continues
  • Standing long is uncomfortable → shorter time or supported alternative
Common mistakes
  • Leaning forward/back
  • Weight mostly on one foot
  • Raised shoulders
  • Holding the breath
  • Stiff knees
  • Chest pushed forward, back over-arched

Teaching point: Tadasana is not just standing straight — it teaches standing with awareness, stability and natural, comfortable alignment.

2
VrikshasanaTree Pose

Vriksha = tree. Standing leg is the trunk, arms are the branches. A balancing pose needing concentration, stability and controlled weight transfer.

Start: Stand in Tadasana · weight even · shoulders relaxed, eyes on a fixed point ahead · keep a wall or chair nearby if needed.

  1. Slowly shift weight onto the left foot; left knee straight but not locked.
  2. Bend the right knee and turn it outward.
  3. Place right heel against the inner left ankle, or sole against the inner calf; if balance and flexibility allow, against the inner thigh.
  4. Never place the foot on the side of the standing knee joint.
  5. Hands together at chest (Namaste).
  6. When steady, raise arms overhead if comfortable.
  7. Pelvis faces forward; don't lean to one side.
  8. Breathe naturally.
  9. Lower arms and foot, return to Tadasana; repeat on the other side.
Breathing: Inhale gently as spine lengthens / arms rise · Natural while balancing · Exhale as arms and foot come downHold: Beginners 10–15 s each side · repeat 2 times per side · increase gradually
Benefits
  • Static balance and coordination
  • Concentration and visual focus
  • Strengthens standing-leg stabilisers
  • Awareness of feet, ankles, hips
  • Upright posture
  • Patience and confidence
Precautions
  • Use wall/chair if balance is hard
  • Avoid one-leg standing if unsafe (injury / major balance problem)
  • Don't press the raised foot hard into the knee
  • Don't force the bent knee outward
  • Hip, knee, ankle problems → comfortable foot position or alternative
  • Stop for pain or dizziness
Common mistakes
  • Foot directly on the standing knee
  • Pelvis shifting sideways
  • Leaning trunk to balance
  • Looking around
  • Arms up before balance is set
  • Holding the breath

Teaching point: Beginners first keep the toes of the raised foot on the floor with the heel against the other ankle; raise the foot higher only when comfortable.

3
UtkatasanaChair Pose

Utkata = powerful / intense / strong. Body looks like sitting on an imaginary chair. Challenges thigh, hip and trunk muscles.

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet together or hip-width · arms at sides · look forward, breathe naturally.

  1. Inhale; raise arms forward or overhead, as comfortable.
  2. Exhale; bend knees gradually.
  3. Hips go back, as if sitting on a chair.
  4. Keep heels on the floor.
  5. Knees bend in line with the toes.
  6. Spine long, chest open — don't force an arch.
  7. Head in line with trunk, look forward.
  8. Hold, breathing normally.
  9. Inhale, straighten legs slowly; lower arms; return to Tadasana.
Breathing: Inhale: raise arms / prepare to bend · Exhale: hips back, knees bend · Normal breathing while holding · Inhale: come upHold: Beginners hold 5–15 s · repeat 2–3 times with rest · start with a shallow bend, deepen only when comfortable.   • Lower-body muscular endurance
  • Leg–trunk coordination
  • Knee and hip alignment awareness
  • Challenges postural stability
  • Strength for sitting down and standing up

  • Lifting heels
  • Leaning too far forward
  • Over-arching lower back
  • Shoulders toward ears
  • Holding the breath

4
TrikonasanaTriangle Pose

Trikona = triangle; legs and trunk make triangle shapes. A standing side-bending pose using legs, hips, trunk and shoulders.

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet wide apart (comfortable) · right foot turned out about 90° · left foot slightly in · arms out sideways at shoulder height · shoulders relaxed, chest open.

  1. Inhale; lengthen spine, arms out sideways.
  2. Exhale; move trunk to the right, hinging mainly at the hip.
  3. Right hand on shin, ankle or a block, per flexibility.
  4. Left arm up if comfortable.
  5. Keep both sides of the trunk long — don't collapse the lower side.
  6. Gently turn chest toward the front, as your mobility allows.
  7. Head neutral, or look up only if comfortable.
  8. Breathe normally.
  9. Inhale, return to standing with control; turn feet and repeat on the left.
Breathing: Inhale: lengthen spine, arms out · Exhale: bend sideways · Natural while holding · Inhale: come upHold: Beginners 10–20 s per side · once or twice per side · use a block or place the lower hand higher if needed
Benefits
  • Sideways movement of the trunk
  • Stretches torso sides; may stretch inner thighs and backs of legs
  • Hip and spine alignment awareness
  • Leg strength and standing stability
  • Arm–leg–trunk coordination
  • Breath awareness in a side bend
Precautions
  • Don't force the hand to the floor
  • Back/hip problems → smaller range or alternative
  • Don't look up if it hurts the neck or makes you dizzy
  • Balance problems → practise near a wall
  • Knees comfortably aligned, not locked
  • Stop for sharp pain or unusual discomfort

  • Bending forward instead of sideways
  • Lower hand too low, trunk collapses
  • Locking knees
  • Over-rotating the body
  • Shoulders toward ears
  • Holding the breath

Teaching point: The hand need not touch the floor. Correct alignment and comfortable movement matter more than depth.

5
KonasanaAngle / Side-Bending Pose

Kona = angle. The name is used for different variations in different traditions; here it is the basic standing side bend.

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet hip-width or slightly wider · weight even · shoulders and arms relaxed · head aligned over trunk.

  1. Inhale; raise the right arm overhead; left arm relaxed at side.
  2. Lengthen the spine gently.
  3. Exhale; bend trunk to the left.
  4. Left hand slides down the outer left thigh if comfortable.
  5. Both feet firmly grounded.
  6. Don't twist the trunk forward or backward.
  7. Chest comfortably open; breathe normally.
  8. Hold a few comfortable breaths.
  9. Inhale, return upright; lower the arm; repeat on the other side.

  • Balanced standing posture

  • Lifting a foot
  • Rotating the chest too much
  • Holding the breath
  • Forcing the arm behind the head

6
ParshvakonasanaExtended Side Angle Pose

Parshva = side, kona = angle. A standing lunge plus a sideways extension of the trunk; teaches leg alignment, trunk length and weight distribution.

  5. Left arm extends overhead: one long line from left foot, along the side, to fingertips.
  6. Chest open — don't force a twist.
  7. Head comfortable; breathe smoothly.
  8. Press through the feet, bring trunk upright.
  9. Straighten the right leg, turn feet back; repeat on the other side.

  • Hip and ankle stability

7
Ardha ChandrasanaHalf Moon Pose

Ardha = half, Chandra = moon; the body forms a half-moon shape. A balancing pose needing coordination, strength, concentration and controlled weight.

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet apart · right foot out, left foot slightly in · arms out sideways · keep a wall or stable support nearby.

  1. Bend right knee slightly; move trunk to the right (like the start of Trikonasana).
  2. Right hand on a block or the floor beside the right foot.
  3. Shift weight gradually onto the right foot.
  4. Lift the left leg slowly behind and to the side, about parallel to the floor, or lower if that is more stable.
  5. Supporting leg comfortably straight, not locked.
  6. Left arm up if comfortable.
  7. Turn the chest gently outward, not forced.
  8. Head comfortable, steady gaze; breathe normally.
  9. Lower the raised leg slowly, return to standing; repeat on the other side.
Breathing: Inhale: lengthen spine, prepare to balance · Exhale: trunk moves toward the supporting side · Smooth breathing while balancing · Breathe naturally as the leg lowers — no breath-holdingHold: Beginners 5–10 s per side · once or twice per side · raised hand against a wall or lower hand on a block if needed

  • Challenges hip and ankle stability
  • Body awareness and concentration
  • Controlled trunk rotation

  • Challenging — introduce gradually

  • Major balance difficulty → simpler alternative
  • Knee, hip, ankle, back injury → get guidance

  • Leg too high, pelvis over-rotates

  • Holding the breath

Teaching point: First learn to transfer weight safely and balance with support. The leg need not reach hip height, and the top arm need not go up until you are stable.

8
HastapadasanaHand-to-Foot Pose / Standing Forward Bend

Hasta = hand, pada = foot. Here: a standing forward bend with hands moving toward the feet (the name varies between traditions).

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet together or hip-width · weight even · shoulders and arms relaxed · knees comfortable, look forward.

  1. Inhale; lengthen the spine.
  2. Exhale; bend forward slowly from the hips, trunk toward thighs.
  3. Knees slightly bent if the backs of the legs are tight.
  4. Hands on shins, ankles, a block or the floor, per flexibility.
  5. Relax the neck — don't pull the head down.
  6. Weight balanced through both feet.
  7. Don't force belly onto thighs or reach for the floor.
  8. Hold comfortably, breathing normally.
  9. To come up: hands on hips/thighs if helpful; bend knees slightly; rise slowly with a long spine; Tadasana.
Breathing: Inhale: lengthen spine · Exhale: fold forward · Natural while holding · Inhale gently: rise with control. Dizzy → come up slowly, avoid repeated head-down movementsHold: Beginners 10–15 s · repeat 1–2 times · bent knees or hand support to reduce strain

  • Stretches backs of the legs (comfortable range)
  • Hip movement
  • Awareness of spine–pelvis–legs relationship
  • May improve flexibility over time
  • Controlled movement and breath attention
  • Practice of a gradual forward bend
Precautions
  • Lower-back pain or recent spinal injury → guidance first
  • No bouncing or pulling deeper with the hands
  • Bend the knees if hamstrings are tight
  • Dizziness or medical conditions → care with head-down positions

  • Stop for sharp pain, numbness, unusual discomfort

  • Bending suddenly from the waist
  • Locking knees
  • Pulling the head toward the legs
  • Forcing hands to the floor
  • Weight pushed to heels or toes
  • Rounding the back too much to reach lower
  • Rising too fast

Teaching point: The aim is controlled forward bending, not touching the toes at any cost. Use bent knees and supports when needed.

9
PadahastasanaHand-under-Foot Pose

Pada = foot, hasta = hand. Hands go under the feet in a standing forward bend. (Hastapadasana brings hands toward the feet; names and techniques vary by school.)

Start: Stand in Tadasana · feet together or comfortable distance · knees relaxed · arms at sides · stable standing position.

  1. Inhale; lengthen the spine.
  2. Exhale; fold forward from the hips.
  3. Bend knees as much as needed to protect lower back and hamstrings.
  4. Slide hands under the feet, palms under soles — only if comfortable.
  5. If you can't reach without strain: hands on shins, ankles or a block.
  6. Neck relaxed, don't pull the head down.
  7. Weight balanced through the feet; breathe naturally.
  8. Hold briefly, comfortably.
  9. Release hands, bend knees, rise slowly with control; Tadasana.

  • Forward-bending mobility
  • Stretches backs of legs when done appropriately
  • Hip-movement and spine-position awareness
  • Patience, gradual flexibility gains
  • Controlled entry/exit of a forward bend
Precautions
  • Don't force hands under the feet
  • Lower-back pain, hamstring injury, recent spinal problems → guidance
  • Bend knees as needed
  • No bouncing, pulling or pressing down with the arms
  • Dizziness → avoid repeated or long head-down
  • Stop for pain, numbness, unusual symptoms

  • Forcing palms under the soles
  • Locking knees with tight hamstrings
  • Pulling the head to the legs
  • Rounding the back too much
  • Moving in or out suddenly
  • Holding the breath

Teaching point: More demanding than a basic forward bend. Learn Hastapadasana comfortably first; try the hand-under-foot version only when suitable. Benefits come from any comfortable forward bend — hands under feet is not required.

GarudasanaEagle Pose

Garuda = the mythical eagle / divine bird. A balancing pose: bent-knee stance, wrapped legs and a special arm position build concentration and coordination.

  7. Bend elbows, forearms up.
  8. Palms together if comfortable; if shoulders are tight, backs of hands together or just cross the arms.
  9. Spine upright, shoulders relaxed, steady gaze, natural breathing.
  10. Release arms first (or with the legs), return to Tadasana; repeat reversing legs and arms.

  • Concentration and visual focus
  • Hip and knee position awareness
  • Challenges shoulder mobility
  • Patience and controlled movement
Precautions
  • Use wall/chair if balance is hard
  • Don't force a deep knee bend
  • Don't wrap the foot if it causes discomfort
  • Knee, ankle, hip, shoulder injuries → modified version or guidance

  • Knees bent too deep

UNIT III

Sitting, Forward-Bending & Twisting Asanas

Sitting asanas are done seated — good for meditation, breathing practice, relaxation and mobility. Forward-bending asanas bend the trunk forward; twisting asanas gently rotate the spine. Move slowly, breathe in control, never force a position. They build flexibility, strength, balance, body awareness and concentration.

General precautions:
  • Practise on a yoga mat, in a clean, well-ventilated space
  • Comfortable clothing; not right after a heavy meal
  • Warm up first: neck, shoulders, hips, knees, spine
  • Steady, comfortable breathing throughout
  • Never bounce, jerk or force a stretch
  • Use cushions, folded blankets or yoga blocks when needed
  • Knee, hip, back or neck injury, recent surgery, or other medical condition → get professional guidance first
  • Stop for sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or unusual discomfort

Sukha = ease, comfort, happiness. A comfortable cross-legged sitting pose.

  1. Sit on the mat, legs straight in front.
  2. Bend knees, bring feet toward the body.
  3. Cross legs comfortably, each foot under the opposite knee or shin.
  4. Hands on knees, palms up or down.
  5. Spine upright, shoulders relaxed, chest open.
  6. Head balanced over shoulders, look straight ahead.
  7. Breathe slowly and naturally.
  8. Stay 1–5 min as comfortable.
  9. Uncross, extend legs gently; repeat with the other leg in front.
Breathing: Slowly and naturally through the nose.Hold: 1–5 min
Benefits
  • Upright sitting posture
  • Concentration and body awareness
  • Relaxation; prepares for meditation and pranayama
  • Gentle awareness of hip and ankle mobility
  • May reduce restlessness during quiet breathing
Precautions
  • Knee or hip discomfort → sit on a folded blanket or cushion
  • Don't force knees toward the floor
  • If cross-legged is uncomfortable → sit on a chair, both feet supported

  4. Hands on thighs, palms down.

Precautions
  • Knee, ankle or foot injury, or significant joint pain → avoid or modify
  • Cushion between hips and heels if uncomfortable
  • Folded blanket under ankles may help
  • Tingling or numbness → come out

PadmasanaLotus PoseAdvanced sitting · meditative

Padma = lotus. Each foot rests on the opposite thigh — a symmetrical seated position.

  1. Sit, legs straight in front.
  2. Bend the right knee; guide the right foot toward the left thigh — only if the hip allows comfortably.
  3. Right foot on left thigh, sole facing up.
  4. Bend left knee; gently guide left foot toward right thigh.
  5. Left foot on right thigh, without twisting or pulling the knee.
  6. Knees relaxed toward the floor, not forced down.
  7. Hands on knees.
  8. Spine upright, shoulders relaxed.

  • Knee, hip or ankle injury or pain → avoid
  • Beginners: Sukhasana or Ardha Padmasana instead

  • Awareness of spinal rotation
  • Mobility of trunk and upper back
  • Gently stretches back and torso muscles
  • Postural control and coordination
  • Mindful movement and controlled breathing
Precautions
  • No forceful twisting; don't use the arms to pull deeper
  • Spinal injury, significant back pain or recent abdominal surgery → professional guidance first
  • Keep the twist gentle; no sudden movements
  • Don't rotate the neck beyond a comfortable range on its own

Teaching point: Rotate gradually from the trunk, keeping the spine long — don't collapse or force the twist.

11
Ardha MatsyendrasanaHalf Lord of the Fishes PoseSitting · twisting

Ardha = half; Matsyendra = a traditional yogic sage. A deeper seated twist than Vakrasana.

  1. Sit, legs straight in front.
  2. Bend right knee; cross right foot over the left leg, outside the left thigh, if comfortable.
  3. Bend the left leg, left foot beside right hip, if comfortable for the knee. Beginners may keep the left leg straight.
  4. Sitting bones grounded as much as possible.
  5. Inhale; lengthen the spine.
  6. Exhale; rotate the trunk to the right.
  7. Right hand on the floor behind you.
  8. Left arm across the body, against the outside of the right thigh or knee.
  9. Spine upright, shoulders relaxed.
  10. Turn the head gently right only if comfortable.
  11. Hold 15–30 s, breathing naturally.
  12. Slowly unwind the torso, release legs.
  13. Repeat on the other side.
Breathing: Inhale to lengthen the spine · exhale to rotate gently · don't hold breath · inhale as you return to centre.Hold: 15–30 s each side
Benefits
  • Spinal rotation and trunk mobility
  • Stretches back, shoulders and torso muscles
  • Upright posture and body awareness
  • Coordinates breathing with movement
  • Practice of controlled spinal rotation
Precautions
  • No forceful twisting, especially with a sensitive lower back
  • Disc conditions, significant back pain, hip/knee injury, or recent spinal/abdominal surgery → professional guidance
  • Don't pull into the twist with the arm
  • Neck relaxed; rotate only within comfort
  • Beginners: keep the lower leg extended — a gentle version

Teaching point: Practise gently on both sides: controlled rotation, spine lengthened, pelvis stable.

UNIT IV

Prone, Supine & Relaxation Asanas

Classified by body position. Practised for flexibility, muscular strength, balance, body awareness and relaxation.

Pronelying on the abdomen, face down — lifting head, chest, arms or legs; builds back and hip strength
Supinelying on the back, face up — may lift legs or trunk, hug knees, twist, or be inverted
Relaxationhelp body and mind settle and recover after effort
General precautions:
  • Firm, comfortable mat; well-ventilated space
  • Gentle warm-up first; not right after a heavy meal
  • Move slowly, breathe naturally; no breath-holding unless a qualified instructor teaches a suitable technique
  • Never force neck, spine, hips or knees
  • Neck/back injury, high blood pressure, glaucoma, pregnancy, recent surgery or other conditions → qualified medical or yoga-therapy guidance before demanding postures
  • Inversions (Halasana, Sarvangasana) need extra care — learn under qualified supervision
  • Stop at once for sharp pain, dizziness, numbness or unusual discomfort

A. Prone asanas

1
BhujangasanaCobra PoseProne · gentle backbend

Bhujanga = snake / cobra; looks like a cobra raising its head and upper body.

  1. Lie on your abdomen, legs extended behind.
  2. Feet together or slightly apart.
  3. Palms on floor beside the chest, elbows bent and close to the body.
  4. Forehead on the mat, shoulders relaxed.
  5. Inhale; lift head and chest using the back muscles; hands give only light support.
  6. Pelvis and lower abdomen stay on the mat.
  7. Don't straighten the elbows fully or push the chest too high.
  8. Shoulders away from ears, neck comfortable.
  9. Hold about 10–20 s, breathing normally.
  10. Exhale; lower chest and forehead slowly.
  11. Relax before repeating.
Breathing: Inhale while lifting the chest, exhale while lowering the body.Hold: 10–20 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens back muscles (when done appropriately)
  • Awareness of spinal extension
  • Gently stretches the front of the torso
  • Better upper-body posture
  • Coordinates movement with breath
Precautions
  • Acute back injury or significant spinal pain → avoid deep backbends
  • Don't force the lower back or push hard through the hands
  • Wrist or shoulder problems → suitable modification
  • Keep neck neutral — don't throw the head back

Teaching point: The lift should be controlled and comfortable. Hands support the movement; they must not force a deep arch.

2
ShalabhasanaLocust PoseProne · strengthening

Shalabha = locust / grasshopper; looks like a locust raising its body.

  1. Lie on your abdomen, legs extended, feet pointing back.
  2. Arms alongside the body, palms down or in.
  3. Forehead or chin rests on the mat; neck comfortable.
  4. Inhale; engage back, buttock and leg muscles.
  5. Slowly lift one or both legs a comfortable distance.
  6. Pelvis grounded; don't swing the legs.
  7. Traditional version keeps arms beside the body; a harder version also lifts the chest.
  8. Shoulders relaxed, neck neutral.
  9. Hold about 5–10 s at first, breathing comfortably.
  10. Exhale; lower legs and chest gently.
  11. Relax before repeating.
Breathing: Inhale while preparing to lift, breathe comfortably during the hold, exhale while lowering.Hold: 5–10 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens back and hip-extensor muscles
  • Control of legs and pelvis
  • Awareness of spinal extension
  • Muscular endurance, when built gradually
  • Postural strength in a balanced programme
Precautions
  • Don't lift legs too high or jerk them up
  • Lower-back pain or recent abdominal/spinal surgery → professional guidance
  • Don't hold the breath during the lift
  • Pose too hard → lower the height or lift one leg at a time

Teaching point: A small, controlled lift beats a high one that compresses or hurts the lower back.

3
DhanurasanaBow PoseProne · backbend · strengthening

Dhanur = bow; the body forms an arch like a bow.

  1. Lie on your abdomen, legs extended, arms alongside.
  2. Bend both knees, heels toward buttocks.
  3. Reach back and hold the ankles or tops of the feet.
  4. Knees about hip-width or slightly narrower.
  5. Inhale; gently lift chest and thighs by using back and leg muscles.
  6. Arms lengthen as you rise — don't yank the ankles.
  7. Neck comfortable; don't throw the head back.
  8. Smooth, natural breathing.
  9. Hold about 5–10 s at first.
  10. Exhale; lower chest and thighs slowly.
  11. Release ankles, relax on the abdomen.
Breathing: Inhale as you gently lift, breathe normally while holding, exhale as you release.Hold: 5–10 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens back and hip muscles
  • Stretches the front of the torso and thighs
  • Upper–lower body coordination
  • Awareness of spinal extension
  • May improve postural strength in a balanced routine
Precautions
  • A harder backbend — introduce gradually
  • Avoid if it causes back pain, or with a significant back injury
  • Caution with knee, shoulder or abdominal conditions
  • Don't pull feet forcefully or spread knees wide
  • Not right after eating

Teaching point: Aim for a smooth, controlled arch. Never pull harder on the ankles to get higher.

4
MakarasanaCrocodile PoseProne · relaxation

Makara = crocodile. A prone relaxation pose; the body rests comfortably on the abdomen.

  1. Lie on your abdomen.
  2. Legs slightly apart, feet turned outward naturally.
  3. Bend elbows, one hand over the other.
  4. Rest forehead on hands — or chin on crossed forearms if easier on the neck.
  5. Or: arms forward, head resting on them.
  6. Relax shoulders, legs and lower back.
  7. Close eyes if comfortable.
  8. Watch the abdomen move gently with each breath.
  9. Stay 1–5 min, or longer if comfortable.
  10. To come out: hands under shoulders, slowly move to a comfortable sitting or lying position.
Breathing: Natural and gentle; watch the abdomen move without forcing deeper breaths.Hold: 1–5 min
Benefits
  • Relaxation after prone strengthening
  • Awareness of natural breathing
  • Back and shoulder muscles rest
  • May reduce muscular tension
  • Quiet pause between harder postures
Precautions
  • Lower-back discomfort → adjust leg position or use another resting pose
  • Avoid an extreme neck angle
  • Pregnant students should not lie on the abdomen — use side-lying relaxation
  • Thin cushion under forehead or chest if needed

Teaching point: Mainly a relaxation pose — don't strain or lift the body in it.

B. Supine / lying asanas

5
Uttan PadasanaRaised Legs PoseSupine · strengthening

Uttan = extended / raised, Pada = foot / leg. Lie on the back and raise the legs (taught in several variations; this is a common straight-leg version).

  1. Lie flat on your back.
  2. Legs extended, together or slightly apart.
  3. Arms alongside, palms down.
  4. Head and shoulders relaxed on the mat.
  5. Inhale; engage the abdominal muscles gently.
  6. Slowly raise both legs together to a comfortable height, knees straight if possible.
  7. Beginners: only 20–30° from the floor.
  8. Keep lower back comfortable, no excessive arch.
  9. Hold 5–10 s at first, breathing normally.
  10. Exhale; lower both legs slowly — don't drop them.
  11. Relax; repeat only if comfortable.
Breathing: Inhale before lifting, exhale slowly while lowering; breathe normally during the hold.Hold: 5–10 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens abdominal muscles and hip flexors
  • Control of leg movement
  • Breath–effort coordination
  • Awareness of pelvis and lower-back position
  • Core endurance, when built gradually
Precautions
  • Lower-back pain → bend the knees or raise one leg at a time
  • Don't lower legs further than you can control
  • No straining or breath-holding
  • After abdominal surgery or any condition limiting abdominal exercise → professional guidance

Teaching point: Control matters more than leg height; the lower back must not become painfully strained.

6
PawanmuktasanaWind-Relieving PoseSupine · flexion · relaxation

Pawan = wind, Mukta = release. Traditionally linked to relieving abdominal gas.

  1. Lie on your back, legs extended.
  2. Bend the right knee, draw it gently toward the chest.
  3. Hold the shin or just below the knee with both hands.
  4. Head and shoulders relaxed on the mat.
  5. Breathe comfortably while holding the knee close.
  6. Slowly release, extend the right leg.
  7. Repeat with the left knee.
  8. After both sides, bend both knees and bring them gently toward the chest.
  9. Hold shins or backs of thighs, whichever is comfortable.
  10. Shoulders relaxed, breathe normally.
  11. Hold the double-knee position about 15–30 s.
  12. Release slowly, return to the start.
Breathing: Slow and natural; let the abdomen move comfortably — don't press it hard against the thighs.Hold: Double-knee hold 15–30 s
Benefits
  • Gently stretches hips and lower-back muscles
  • Comfortable movement of the lower spine
  • Awareness of abdominal breathing
  • May ease gas / abdominal fullness for some (varies)
  • Gentle relaxation after activity
Precautions
  • Don't compress the abdomen forcefully
  • Recent abdominal surgery, painful hernia, severe abdominal discomfort or pregnancy → guidance first
  • Keep the head on the mat — don't pull it to the knees (unless a separately taught variation)
  • Stop if it hurts or worsens abdominal symptoms

Teaching point: Knees come toward the chest only as far as comfortable; gentle pressure is enough.

7
Setu BandhasanaBridge PoseSupine · backbend · strengthening

Setu = bridge, Bandha = formation / lock. The body forms a bridge as the hips rise.

  1. Lie on your back, knees bent.
  2. Feet flat, about hip-width apart.
  3. Heels a comfortable distance from the hips.
  4. Arms alongside, palms down.
  5. Head and shoulders rest on the mat.
  6. Inhale; press gently through the feet.
  7. Slowly lift hips — pelvis and lower back — into a comfortable bridge.
  8. Knees point forward, about parallel.
  9. Don't turn the head while hips are lifted.
  10. Hold 10–20 s, breathing normally.
  11. Exhale; lower spine and hips to the mat.
  12. Rest briefly before repeating.
Breathing: Inhale as you lift the hips, natural while holding, exhale as you lower.Hold: 10–20 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens glutes and backs of the legs
  • Engages trunk and pelvis support muscles
  • Gently stretches front of hips and torso
  • Awareness of hip extension and spine position
  • Muscular endurance in a balanced routine
Precautions
  • Don't lift hips too high
  • Neck injury → professional guidance first
  • Don't turn the head side to side while holding
  • Knees aligned — not falling out or collapsing in
  • Stop for back, neck or knee pain

Teaching point: Spread the lift comfortably through hips and trunk; avoid heavy pressure on the neck.

8
NaukasanaBoat Pose (supine)Supine · abdominal strengthening

Nauka = boat. Raised legs and upper body make a boat shape. (Taught in seated and supine forms — this is the supine version.)

  1. Lie on your back, legs extended, arms alongside.
  2. Head and shoulders relaxed at first.
  3. Inhale; gently engage the abdominal muscles.
  4. Exhale; slowly raise legs and upper body a comfortable distance.
  5. Arms reach toward the legs, about parallel to the floor.
  6. Neck in line with the spine; don't pull the head forward.
  7. Beginners: bend knees, or lift only slightly.
  8. Hold about 5–10 s, without holding the breath.
  9. Exhale; lower legs, shoulders and head slowly.
  10. Rest before repeating.
Breathing: Steady during the hold; no breath-holding or straining.Hold: 5–10 s
Benefits
  • Strengthens abdominal muscles and hip flexors
  • Core control and endurance
  • Upper–lower body coordination
  • Awareness of trunk stability
  • Builds strength for other activities when practised progressively
Precautions
  • Puts big demand on abdominals and lower back
  • Back pain, abdominal hernia, recent abdominal surgery → professional advice first
  • Don't jerk the head or pull the neck forward
  • Don't force straight legs if the lower back arches painfully
  • Start with a shorter hold and smaller lift

Teaching point: Strengthen the core through controlled effort — not through breath-holding or excessive strain.

9
Supta MatsyendrasanaSupine Spinal TwistSupine · twisting · relaxation

Supta = lying down / reclining; Matsyendra = a traditional yogic sage. A reclining spinal twist.

  1. Lie on your back, legs extended.
  2. Bend both knees toward the chest.
  3. Arms out at shoulder height, palms up.
  4. Exhale; slowly lower both knees to the right.
  5. Gentle movement; shoulders stay comfortably on the mat.
  6. Turn head to the left only if the neck is comfortable.
  7. Cushion under knees if they don't reach the floor.
  8. Hold 20–30 s, breathing normally.
  9. Inhale; bring knees back to centre.
  10. Exhale; lower knees to the left; hold similar time.
  11. Return to centre, extend legs, relax.
Breathing: Exhale while lowering the knees; normal breathing while holding; inhale to return to centre.Hold: 20–30 s each side
Benefits
  • Gently stretches trunk and hip muscles
  • Comfortable rotation of the spine
  • Relaxation after strengthening
  • Awareness of spine and pelvic movement
  • May ease muscular stiffness when gentle
Precautions
  • Don't force knees to the floor
  • Stay in comfortable range, especially with a sensitive lower back
  • Don't twist the neck beyond comfort
  • Spinal injury, recent surgery or significant back pain → professional guidance first

Teaching point: A gentle twist — knees go down only as far as back and hips comfortably allow.

Relaxation asana

10
ShavasanaCorpse PoseRelaxation

Shava = corpse. Lie still, relax the body, keep awareness.

  1. Lie flat on your back on a comfortable mat.
  2. Legs extended, feet fall outward naturally.
  3. Arms slightly away from the body, palms up if comfortable.
  4. Adjust head and neck so they feel supported.
  5. Close eyes gently, if comfortable.
  6. Relax forehead, jaw, shoulders, arms, abdomen, hips and legs.
  7. Let breathing stay natural — don't control it.
  8. Watch the breath, or move awareness slowly through body parts.
  9. Stay 5–10 min, or as the session needs.
  10. To finish: deepen the breath gently, move fingers and toes.
  11. Turn onto one side, pause, then slowly sit up.
Breathing: Let it flow naturally; no forced deep breathing, no breath-holding.Hold: 5–10 min
Benefits
  • Physical and mental relaxation
  • Helps you settle after hard activity
  • Awareness of breath and body sensations
  • May reduce perceived stress and muscle tension
  • Practice of mindful attention
Precautions
  • Lower-back discomfort → bolster or pillow under the knees
  • Small folded blanket can support the head
  • Pregnant students (especially later) may prefer side-lying relaxation
  • If lying still is uncomfortable → eyes open or a supported position

Teaching point: Shavasana is not just sleeping — relax the body while keeping gentle awareness.

Inverted asanas — advanced, learn under qualified supervision

HalasanaPlough PoseSupine · inverted · forward-folding

Hala = plough; legs go overhead like a traditional plough.

  1. Lie on your back, legs extended, arms alongside.
  2. Head and neck neutral.
  3. Inhale; prepare gently, shoulders relaxed.
  4. Raise legs toward vertical only if you can without strain.
  5. With proper instruction and support, lift hips and take legs overhead.
  6. Feet go toward the floor behind the head only if comfortable and within ability.
  7. Weight on shoulders and upper back, not the neck.
  8. Don't turn the head.
  9. Smooth breathing; don't force the position.
  10. To come out: hands support the back if needed; lower spine and legs with control.
  11. Rest on your back before the next posture.
Breathing: Slow and natural; never hold the breath or strain to stay.Hold: As trained / supervised
Benefits
  • Awareness of spinal and hip movement
  • Stretches muscles along the back of the body
  • Challenges flexibility and postural control
  • May help concentration and controlled movement
  • Part of advanced practice under qualified supervision
Precautions
  • Advanced — don't attempt without instruction
  • Heavy stress on the neck (cervical spine): never put weight on the neck or force feet to the floor
  • Avoid with neck/spinal problems, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma or other conditions unsuited to inversions — unless cleared by a professional
  • Osteoporosis or past spinal injury → specialist guidance
  • No momentum to throw the legs over
  • Don't turn the head while holding

Teaching point: Not suitable for every student. Beginners learn preparatory movements first and are never pressured to reach the full pose.

12
SarvangasanaShoulder StandInverted

Sarva = all, Anga = limbs / body parts. Traditionally called the Shoulder Stand.

  1. Lie on your back on a mat.
  2. Legs together, arms alongside.
  3. Inhale; raise the legs gradually toward vertical, if comfortable.
  4. With instruction, lift hips and trunk (legs briefly overhead for a controlled entry).
  5. Hands on the back for support, elbows on the mat.
  6. Slowly raise the legs toward the ceiling; keep the body as aligned as comfortably possible.
  7. Weight on shoulders and upper arms — never the neck.
  8. Don't turn the head.
  9. Breathe comfortably; hold only as long as your training and supervision allow.
  10. To release: lower legs with control, bring the spine gradually back to the mat.
  11. Rest quietly before another posture.
Breathing: Slow and natural. Stop if you can't breathe comfortably.Hold: As trained / supervised
Benefits
  • Body awareness, balance, posture control
  • Challenges shoulder-girdle and trunk stability
  • Needs coordination and controlled movement
  • May belong to advanced traditional practice when suitable
Precautions
  • Advanced inversion — learn from a qualified instructor
  • Big demand on the neck and cervical spine
  • Avoid with neck/spinal injury, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma or other conditions unsuited to inversions — without professional clearance
  • Osteoporosis or past spinal injury → no attempt without specialist guidance
  • Never turn the head side to side
  • No jerky lifting or lowering
  • Don't force a perfectly vertical line

UNIT V

Surya Namaskar

Sun Salutation

Surya = Sun, Namaskar = salutation / respectful greeting. A flowing sequence of yoga postures joined with controlled breathing and concentration; traditionally also reverence for the Sun. Usually practised in the morning, but any suitable time works if you've left enough time after eating. Used as a warm-up and as a complete sequence — it moves arms, legs, spine, shoulders, hips and trunk.

Objectives
  • Flexibility and mobility throughout the body
  • Muscular strength and endurance
  • Coordinate breathing with movement
  • Body awareness, balance, concentration
  • Prepare the body for other asanas
  • Relaxation and mindful movement
  • A regular, disciplined practice
Preparation before practice
  1. Clean, non-slip yoga mat
  2. Comfortable clothing for free movement
  3. Well-ventilated space with enough room
  4. Not right after a heavy meal — wait about 2–3 hours after a large meal
  5. Start with gentle neck, shoulder, wrist, hip, knee and ankle movements
  6. Learn correct alignment of each posture before speeding up
  7. Coordinate each movement with breathing; don't hold the breath
  8. Beginners: 2–4 comfortable rounds, then increase gradually

Why it is important: Combines forward bends, backward extensions, lunges, plank-like positions and inverted-V positions in one sequence. At a suitable pace it gives both mobility work and physical exercise. Traditionally also a symbolic expression of gratitude to the Sun, which sustains life on Earth.

Precautions: Wrist, shoulder, knee or back injury → modify the sequence. Balance difficulty or medical conditions affecting exercise tolerance → professional guidance. Pregnant students → learn pregnancy-appropriate modifications from a qualified instructor, not the standard sequence. Stop for sharp pain, dizziness, chest discomfort or unusual shortness of breath.

The 12 steps

A commonly taught traditional form: 12 positions in a continuous sequence, each matched with an inhale, exhale or brief natural pause.

1PranamasanaPrayer Pose
Exhale / natural

Stand at the front of the mat, feet together or close · weight even · spine upright, shoulders relaxed, gaze forward · palms together at the chest · face relaxed, calm breath.

Benefits: Standing-posture awareness; concentration; prepares the mind.

2Hasta UttanasanaRaised Arms Pose

Raise both arms overhead (close to ears if comfortable) · lengthen spine, lift chest gently · if suitable, lean slightly back without squeezing the lower back · legs stable, shoulders relaxed.

3PadahastasanaStanding Forward Bend
Exhale

Bend forward from the hips, trunk toward thighs · hands beside feet or on shins · knees slightly bent if hamstrings are tight · neck relaxed, don't pull down · smooth, controlled.

Benefits: Stretches backs of legs; hip mobility; forward-bend awareness.

Precaution: don't force head to knees or round the lower back too much.

4Ashwa SanchalanasanaEquestrian Pose

Benefits: Strengthens arms, shoulders, abdomen and trunk.

Modification: both knees down, keeping a straight line from head through hips to knees.

6Ashtanga NamaskaraEight-Point Salute
Exhale / brief natural pause

From plank, lower both knees to the mat · bend elbows, lower chest and chin toward the floor · hips slightly raised · 8 points touch the ground: 2 feet, 2 knees, 2 hands, chest, chin · neck comfortable, don't drop suddenly.

Benefits: Body awareness; controlled lowering of the upper body.

Precaution: wrist, shoulder or neck discomfort → modified lowering.

7BhujangasanaCobra Pose
Inhale

Slide forward gently · tops of feet on the mat, pelvis grounded · palms beside the chest · lift head and chest using back muscles, hands lightly supporting · elbows slightly bent, shoulders relaxed, neck comfortable.

Benefits: Strengthens back muscles; stretches the front of the trunk; spinal-extension control.

Precaution: don't push the chest too high or compress the lower back.

8ParvatasanaMountain Pose
Exhale

Press through hands, lift hips up and back · toes tucked, legs extended as comfortable · body makes an inverted V · spine long, head between upper arms without forcing the neck · heels may stay lifted.

Benefits: Stretches backs of legs and shoulders; upper-body support; coordination.

Precaution: bend the knees slightly if needed to keep the spine long.

9Ashwa SanchalanasanaEquestrian Pose (other side)
Inhale

From Mountain Pose bring the right foot forward between the hands · right foot flat, right knee bent · lower left knee to floor if needed · chest forward, look ahead · hands support; adjust the stance if the foot doesn't reach easily.

Benefits: Stretches the front of the rear hip; balance; coordination.

Teaching point: opposite-side lunge to Step 4, so both sides are practised.

10PadahastasanaStanding Forward Bend
Exhale

Left foot forward to meet the right · feet close · fold forward from the hips · hands beside feet or on shins · knees slightly bent if needed, neck relaxed.

Benefits: Stretches backs of legs; controlled hip and spine movement.

Precaution: no jerking the trunk or forcing hands to the floor.

11Hasta UttanasanaRaised Arms Pose
Inhale

Rise slowly from the bend · sweep both arms forward and up overhead · lengthen spine, lift chest gently · abdomen comfortably engaged · only a small backward extension, if comfortable.

Benefits: Mobilises shoulders; stretches the front of the trunk; returns the body upright.

Precaution: rise slowly if prone to dizziness.

12PranamasanaPrayer Pose
Exhale

Trunk back to neutral, upright · lower both arms slowly · palms together at the chest · feet grounded, shoulders relaxed, spine upright · pause briefly and watch the breath.

Benefits: Concentration, calmness, awareness of the body's response to movement.

Teaching point: completes the first half-cycle.

How to complete a full round

In the 12-step sequence above, Step 4 takes the right leg back and Step 9 brings that same leg forward. To balance both sides:

  1. Do Steps 1–12, starting Step 4 with the right leg going back.
  2. Repeat the sequence, starting Step 4 with the left leg going back.
  3. Keep breathing steady and a comfortable pace.

These two sides together make one balanced round in many versions. Some schools count each 12-step sequence as one round — follow your instructor's counting.

Benefits depend on health, technique, pace and consistency. Surya Namaskar must not be presented as a cure for any disease.
UNIT VI

Pranayama & Breathing Practices

Pranayama = Prana (vital life force) + Ayama (expansion / regulation): regulating, becoming aware of, and controlling the breath. It builds awareness of breathing patterns, concentration, relaxation, and physical-mental balance. Different techniques give relaxing, cooling, balancing, controlled or energising effects. You must know the technique, breathing pattern, benefits and precautions of each prescribed practice and demonstrate it correctly in the practical exam.

Before practising pranayama
  1. Clean, quiet, well-ventilated place
  2. Sit steadily — Sukhasana, Padmasana, Vajrasana or another stable seat — spine upright
  3. Relax shoulders, face and abdomen (unless a technique needs controlled muscle use)
  4. Empty or light stomach, preferably before meals
  5. Breathe gently and naturally — never force or strain
  6. Beginners: no long breath retention unless taught and supervised by a qualified instructor
  7. Stop at once for dizziness, chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness or discomfort
  8. Asthma, significant respiratory/cardiovascular conditions, uncontrolled blood pressure or other concerns → medical advice before forceful or specialised techniques

A. Foundational practices

1Breath Awareness

Observing natural breathing without changing its speed, depth or rhythm. Builds mindfulness; the base of all other pranayama.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright, body relaxed.
  2. Close eyes gently, if comfortable.
  3. Hands on thighs, or one hand on the abdomen.
  4. Watch natural inhalation and exhalation.
  5. Notice abdomen, ribs and chest moving.
  6. Notice if the breath is slow, fast, shallow or deep — without judging.
  7. Continue about 2–5 min.
Breathing pattern

Natural, spontaneous, unforced. No deliberate retention.

Benefits
  • Awareness of breathing habits
  • Calms the mind, improves concentration
  • Relaxation and mindfulness
  • Spot tension and irregular breathing
  • Prepares for other pranayama
Precautions
  • Don't force the breath to be slow or deep
  • Eyes uncomfortable closed → keep them softly open
  • Anxious while watching the breath → shift attention to your feet touching the ground
2Diaphragmatic Breathing / Dirgha Pranayama

Diaphragmatic breathing stresses diaphragm / abdominal movement. Dirgha (three-part or complete yogic breathing) adds awareness of abdomen, rib cage and upper chest. Related but not identical.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably or lie on your back.
  2. One hand on the abdomen, the other on the upper chest.
  3. Inhale gently through the nose; abdomen expands naturally.
  4. Continue the inhale: lower ribs and chest expand comfortably.
  5. Exhale slowly; chest and abdomen relax.
  6. Don't lift the shoulders much or force air in.
  7. 5–10 comfortable breathing cycles.
Breathing pattern

Inhale: abdomen expands, then ribs and upper chest (three-part). Exhale: relaxed release, chest and abdomen return toward rest. Retention: not required for beginners.

Benefits
  • Awareness of breathing mechanics
  • Efficient, relaxed breathing
  • Less needless tension in shoulders and upper chest
  • May promote relaxation and reduce perceived stress
  • Base for other techniques
Precautions
  • No exaggerated abdominal movement
  • Don't force a deep inhale or long exhale
  • Stop if breathing is uncomfortable or you feel dizzy
3Omkar Pranayama

Breathing + chanting Om / Aum: controlled exhalation, vocal vibration and focused attention.

Technique
  1. Sit in a comfortable meditative posture, spine upright.
  2. Eyes gently closed if comfortable; relax face and shoulders.
  3. Comfortable inhale through the nose.
  4. Exhale slowly while chanting “Om” — smooth, steady voice.
  5. Let the sound flow; no throat strain.
  6. Traditionally: starts with “A”, then “U”, then humming “M”.
  7. At the end, stay quiet a moment and notice sensations.
  8. Repeat 5–9 rounds, or as the instructor says.
Breathing pattern

Gentle nasal inhale · slow controlled exhale with chanting · natural breathing between rounds · no forced retention.

Benefits
  • Relaxation and mental calmness
  • Concentration and awareness
  • Steady exhalation
  • Soothing focus from vocal vibration
  • Can be added to meditation and relaxation
Precautions
  • Don't strain the voice or be too loud
  • Don't force the exhale to stretch the chant
  • Throat discomfort → practise gently or avoid chanting until comfortable
  • No breathlessness
Practical exam points
  • Correct seated posture
  • Comfortable inhalation
  • Smooth, audible Om during exhalation
  • Relaxed face and shoulders
  • Calm pause between rounds

B. Balancing & relaxation practices

4Anulom Vilom Pranayama

Alternate-nostril breathing: inhale and exhale alternately through the two nostrils. In basic teaching, done without retention.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright.
  2. Left hand on left knee.
  3. Right hand raised; fingers gently control the nostrils.
  4. Close right nostril → inhale through the left.
  5. Close left, release right → exhale through the right.
  6. Inhale through the right.
  7. Close right → exhale through the left.
  8. That is 1 round. Do 5–10 rounds, smooth and unstrained.
Breathing pattern

Left inhale → Right exhale → Right inhale → Left exhale.

Benefits
  • Awareness and control of breath
  • Steady, rhythmic breathing
  • May support relaxation and concentration
  • Gentle preparation for meditation
Precautions
  • Don't block the nostrils forcefully
  • No retention unless instructed and medically suitable
  • Blocked nose → don't force air
  • Stop for dizziness or discomfort
5Nadi Shodhana Pranayama

Alternate-nostril breathing traditionally said to balance and purify the subtle energy channels (Nadis). Often used interchangeably with Anulom Vilom; some traditions separate them by sequence, retention or ratios.

Technique
  1. Sit stable and comfortable, spine upright, shoulders relaxed.
  2. Right hand controls the nostrils gently.
  3. Close right nostril, inhale through the left.
  4. Close left nostril, exhale through the right.
  5. Inhale through the right.
  6. Close right, exhale through the left.
  7. Repeat 5–10 rounds.
Breathing pattern

Alternate inhale and exhale through left and right, slow comfortable rhythm.

Benefits
  • Calm, attentive breathing
  • May promote relaxation and mental focus
  • Breath coordination and awareness
  • Preparation for meditation
Precautions
  • Beginners: no retention
  • Don't force a fixed ratio
  • Don't practise through a completely blocked nostril
  • Advanced versions with retention → qualified supervision

6Bhramari Pranayama

Humming Bee Breath (Bhramari = bee): a gentle humming sound during exhalation.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright.
  2. Close eyes gently, relax the face.
  3. Inhale slowly through the nose.
  4. Exhale through the nose with a smooth hum, like a bee.
  5. Mouth closed, jaw relaxed.
  6. Feel the gentle vibration around face and head.
  7. Repeat 5–9 rounds.
Breathing pattern

Gentle nasal inhale · slow nasal exhale with humming · next inhale comes naturally. (Ears may be lightly covered with fingers in some traditional variations — not needed for the basic exam technique.)

Benefits
  • Relaxation and mental calmness
  • Attention on sound and vibration
  • Smooth, controlled exhalation
  • May reduce perceived stress and restlessness
  • Useful before meditation or relaxation
Precautions
  • Not a loud or strained hum
  • No forceful inhale/exhale
  • Don't press the ears hard
  • Stop for dizziness, headache, discomfort
  • Sensitive to sound or ear pressure → practise gently
Practical exam points
  • Stable seated posture
  • Gentle nasal inhalation
  • Continuous humming on exhalation
  • Relaxed face, closed mouth
  • Smooth breathing without strain

C. Cooling practices

7Sheetali Pranayama

A traditional cooling pranayama (Sheetali = coolness): inhale through a rolled tongue, exhale through the nose.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright.
  2. Relax shoulders and face.
  3. Stick the tongue out slightly; roll its sides up into a tube (if physically possible).
  4. Inhale slowly through the rolled tongue.
  5. Withdraw tongue, close mouth.
  6. Exhale gently through the nose.
  7. Repeat 5–10 rounds, or as directed.
Breathing pattern

Inhale through the rolled tongue · exhale through the nose · no retention for beginners.

Benefits
  • Cooling feeling on inhalation
  • May promote relaxation
  • Mindful, controlled breathing
  • Useful in warm conditions, if practised suitably
Precautions
  • Avoid or modify in very cold conditions
  • Don't breathe in through the mouth if the air is dusty, polluted or irritating
  • Can't roll the tongue → do Sheetkari
  • No forced inhale or long retention
  • Stop for coughing, throat irritation or dizziness
Practical exam points
  • Correct seated posture
  • Tongue rolled into a tube
  • Gentle inhalation through the tongue
  • Exhalation through the nose
  • Relaxed, controlled breathing
8Sheetkari Pranayama

A cooling alternative to Sheetali: inhale through slightly parted teeth, often with a soft hissing sound.

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright.
  2. Lips slightly open, teeth gently together or nearly together.
  3. Draw the breath slowly through the gaps between the teeth.
  4. Close the mouth after inhaling.
  5. Exhale gently through the nose.
  6. Repeat 5–10 rounds.
Breathing pattern

Inhale through the teeth, mouth slightly open · exhale through the nose · no forced retention.

Benefits
  • Cooling sensation
  • Mindful breathing
  • May support relaxation
  • Alternative for people who can't roll the tongue
Precautions

  • Tooth sensitivity → may be uncomfortable
  • Avoid dusty or polluted air
  • Don't clamp the teeth tightly
  • Stop for discomfort, coughing or dizziness
Practical exam points
  • Correct seated posture
  • Proper teeth and lip position
  • Gentle inhale through the teeth
  • Exhale through the nose
  • Smooth, unforced rhythm

D. Controlled breathing practices

9Ujjayi Pranayama

Controlled breathing with a gentle narrowing of the throat that makes a soft, ocean-like sound. Ujjayi is linked with “victory” (victorious breath).

Technique
  1. Sit comfortably, spine upright.
  2. Relax shoulders, jaw and face.
  3. Inhale slowly through the nose.
  4. Gently narrow the throat, as if softly fogging a mirror — mouth closed.
  5. Keep breathing through the nose with a subtle, smooth throat sound.
  6. Exhale slowly through the nose with the same gentle throat control.
  7. 5–10 comfortable cycles.
Breathing pattern

Slow controlled nasal inhale and exhale · gentle throat sound on both · no retention for beginners.

Linked with the solar / warming side of yogic breathing: inhale through the right nostril, exhale through the left.

  3. Close the left nostril.

  6. Exhale slowly through the left.
  7. 5–10 comfortable rounds.

E. Energising practices

12Bhastrika Pranayama

Bellows Breath: active inhalation and exhalation, more vigorous than ordinary breathing.

Technique
  1. Sit upright and stable; shoulders relaxed, spine comfortably erect.
  2. Begin with a few normal breaths.
  3. Inhale actively through the nose; chest and abdomen move naturally.
  4. Exhale actively through the nose, without too much force.
  5. Continue a short series of controlled breaths at a pace suited to your ability.
  6. Return to normal breathing and rest after the round.
  7. Practise only under qualified instruction, especially at first.
Breathing pattern

Both inhalation and exhalation are active. (Kapalabhati: active exhale, passive inhale.)

Benefits
  • Awareness of respiratory effort and coordination
  • May give a feeling of alertness
  • Practice of controlled, active breathing
  • Traditionally energising
Precautions
  • Not for everyone; beginners must not practise it forcefully
  • Avoid unsupervised if pregnant, or with uncontrolled high blood pressure, significant heart disease, history of stroke, or other conditions where forceful breathing is unsafe
  • No long retention
  • Stop at once for dizziness, tingling, headache, chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness
  • Never compete on speed or intensity
13Kapalabhati Pranayama

A cleansing breathing practice. Kapala = skull / forehead, Bhati = shining. Active, fairly sharp exhalations with passive inhalations. Some traditions class it as a cleansing practice (Shatkarma).

Technique
  1. Sit upright, spine straight, shoulders relaxed.
  2. Start with a few normal breaths.
  3. Gently contract the abdominal muscles → a short, active exhale through the nose.
  4. Let inhalation happen naturally as the abdomen relaxes.
  5. Repeat at a slow, manageable pace for a short series.
  6. Stop active breathing, return to normal breathing.
  7. Rest before another round.
  8. Beginners: learn under qualified supervision — no rapid or forceful repetitions alone.
Breathing pattern

Active exhale through the nose · passive natural inhale · short cycles, then normal breathing.

Benefits
  • Awareness of abdominal control in exhalation
  • Coordination of abdominal movement with breath
  • Traditionally a cleansing practice
  • May give a feeling of alertness
Precautions
  • No forceful or very rapid repetitions
  • Beginners: not for long periods
  • Avoid in pregnancy; medical guidance for uncontrolled high blood pressure, significant heart disease, history of stroke, abdominal hernia, recent abdominal surgery or other conditions
  • Stop for dizziness, headache, chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness
  • Don't confuse with Bhastrika: Kapalabhati = active exhale; Bhastrika = active inhale and exhale

UNIT VII

Relaxation, Concentration & Meditation

Stress from studies, exams, competition, responsibilities and too much screen time can affect sleep, attention, memory and emotional well-being. Yoga gives simple tools: Shavasana, breath awareness, body scan, Om meditation, mindfulness and concentration exercises. Relaxation releases physical and mental tension · concentration focuses the mind on one object or activity · meditation builds deeper awareness and calmness. Regular practice supports emotional balance, self-awareness, attention and well-being.

1. Shavasana (Corpse Pose)

In one line: Shava = corpse. Body still and relaxed, like lying down peacefully — but with gentle awareness. Looks simple; needs conscious relaxation of body and mind.
Procedure
  1. Lie flat on your back on a mat or comfortable firm surface.
  2. Legs slightly apart, feet fall outward.
  3. Arms beside body, a little away from torso, palms up.
  4. Close eyes gently if comfortable.
  5. Let the whole body rest, no unnecessary movement.
  6. Watch natural breathing; don't control it.
  7. Relax feet, legs, abdomen, shoulders, arms, neck, face.
  8. Let thoughts come and go without getting involved.
  9. Stay about 5–10 min (as comfortable).
  10. To finish: deepen breath gently, move fingers and toes, turn to one side, sit up slowly.
Benefits
  • Reduces physical tension; relaxation
  • Calmness; may reduce stress
  • Notice needless muscle tension
  • Rest after exercise or yoga
  • Awareness of breath and sensations
  • May support better sleep (as part of a relaxing routine)
  • Patience; ability to stay still
Precautions
  • Quiet, comfortable, ventilated place
  • Pillow under knees or head if needed
  • Lying on the back uncomfortable → supported side-lying
  • Don't fall asleep on purpose — aim is conscious relaxation

Educational importance: rest is essential for health; teaches the difference between sleeping and consciously relaxing while staying aware.

2. Yogic Relaxation

Meaning: consciously releasing physical, mental and emotional tension through awareness, breathing and stillness. Unlike ordinary rest (where you may stay distracted by thoughts), you deliberately bring attention to body and mind. Can be done lying, sitting or in another suitable posture.

Procedure (about 5–15 min): quiet place, sit or lie comfortably → eyes closed if comfortable → a few gentle natural breaths → attention to feet, release tension → move attention up: legs, hips, abdomen, chest, shoulders, arms, neck, face → notice tight muscles and let them soften → watch breathing without forcing deeper/slower → thoughts arise: acknowledge, return to relaxation → finish slowly by moving gently and opening eyes.

Benefits: recovery from daily demands · less perceived stress and restlessness · awareness of tension and emotions · self-control and patience · readiness for study or work · general well-being with sleep, activity and healthy habits. For students: long sitting, studying and screens cause muscle tension and mental fatigue — yogic relaxation is a pause to release tension and return with more comfort and awareness.

3. Breath Awareness

Meaning: observing natural breathing without changing its speed, depth or rhythm. Builds present-moment awareness and helps you notice changes linked to tension, excitement or calm. One of the simplest introductory practices in yoga and meditation.

Procedure (3–10 min): sit on chair/floor with spine naturally upright (or lie down) → relax shoulders, arms, face → eyes closed or gaze lowered → attention to the breath → feel air entering and leaving the nostrils → feel chest or abdomen move → notice natural length and rhythm of each inhale and exhale → no forced deep breaths, no holding → mind wanders: bring it back to the next breath.

Benefits: present-moment awareness · notice breathing changes with emotion · may reduce stress and restlessness · patience and sustained attention · a pause before reacting · foundation for advanced meditation.

Example: nervous before an exam and breathing fast? Don't react — watch the breath for a minute or two to settle. Precautions: no retention, forceful or very deep breathing; dizziness/breathlessness/discomfort → normal breathing, stop if it continues.

4. Body Scan & Body Awareness

Body scan: a mindfulness technique that moves attention systematically through the body, noticing warmth, coolness, pressure, tingling, tension, relaxation, floor contact — with curiosity and acceptance, not judging or fixing every sensation. Body awareness: the ability to recognise sensations, posture, movement, muscle tension and other physical signals (e.g. tight shoulders after long sitting; tense jaw when anxious).

Procedure (5–15 min; shorter version for class breaks): lie on a mat or sit relaxed → natural breathing → toes and feet → notice sensations without judging → ankles, calves, knees, thighs → hips, abdomen, chest, back → hands, fingers, arms, shoulders → neck, jaw, cheeks, eyes, forehead → feel the body as a whole → return to surroundings.

5. Om Meditation

Meaning: attention on the sound, vibration or silent repetition of the sacred syllable Om (Aum). In Indian traditions Om is a sacred sound linked with consciousness and the deeper nature of existence; it steadies attention and brings inner stillness. Can be chanted aloud, repeated softly, or recalled mentally in silence.

Procedure (5–10 rounds, or a few minutes): sit comfortably, back naturally upright → shoulders relaxed, hands on thighs/knees → eyes closed if comfortable → 2–3 natural breaths → inhale comfortably → on the exhale chant “Om” slowly and gently (optional) → let the sound end naturally, no voice strain → notice sound and vibrations → pause and notice the silence after each repetition → or repeat Om silently in the mind → finish sitting quietly a little before opening eyes.

Benefits: a single point of focus · relaxation, calmness · awareness of sound and breath · regular meditation and self-reflection · prepares for tasks needing attention · introduces an important traditional yoga practice. Precautions: comfortable volume and pitch · don't force the voice or stretch the exhale · prefer not to chant → silent repetition or breath awareness · respect individual beliefs and cultural preferences in class. Importance: shows how sound, repetition, silence and steady awareness work in meditation.

6. Basic Mindfulness Practice

Mindfulness = paying attention to the present moment on purpose, with openness, curiosity and non-judgment — noticing body and mind without calling an experience good or bad. It does not mean stopping all thoughts or an empty mind; you notice thoughts, feelings and sensations and gently return to the present activity.

6 elements: present-moment awareness · non-judgment · acceptance · patience · awareness of thoughts and emotions (without automatically acting) · intentional attention (choose where it goes).

Procedure (3–5 min at first): sit comfortably in a quiet place → settle, natural breath → choose one thing: breath, sounds around you, or feet on the floor → pay attention to it as it happens → notice when the mind goes to past, future or other topics → acknowledge without criticism → gently return → slowly lengthen the time → at the end notice surroundings, resume activity mindfully.

7. Concentration Practice

Concentration = directing attention to one object, thought, activity or task for a period while reducing needless distraction. In yoga it relates to Dharana (fixing the mind on a chosen point or object). Learning needs it: attend to information, understand, remember, finish tasks accurately.

8. Basic Meditation Technique

Meditation = training attention and awareness for a more stable, clear and balanced relationship with thoughts, feelings and experience. In yoga it relates to Dhyana (a sustained flow of attention toward a chosen focus). Methods: watch the breath, repeat a mantra, attend to body sensations, or rest in open awareness. The mind need not become blank — thoughts come, and you notice them without being absorbed.

  1. Place — quiet and comfortable, few interruptions.
  2. Posture — chair or floor, spine naturally upright, not stiff.
  3. Relax — soften shoulders, hands, jaw, face.
  4. Settle attention — eyes closed or gaze lowered, if comfortable.
  5. Choose a focus — natural breath, a mantra, or a body sensation.
  6. Observe — pay attention to the focus; don't force anything.
  7. Recognise wandering — notice gently when the mind goes to memories, plans, worries.
  8. Return — bring attention back, no self-blame.
  9. Continue — start with 3–5 min, lengthen if comfortable.
  10. End slowly — notice body and surroundings before resuming activity.

Benefits: may reduce perceived stress, calm · awareness of thoughts, feelings, sensations · patience and non-reactivity · attention and emotional regulation · self-reflection · general psychological well-being with consistent practice.

10. Mental Calmness & Concentration — why they matter

Mental calmness = relative emotional steadiness; thinking and responding without being overwhelmed by stress, fear, anger or worry. It does not mean no emotions — you can still feel excitement, sadness, anxiety or frustration but recognise them and respond thoughtfully.
  1. Manage stress — respond better to academic/personal pressure
  2. Emotional regulation — recognise emotions before reacting
  3. Clear thinking — easier to organise thoughts, find solutions
  4. Healthy relationships — listen patiently, respond thoughtfully
  5. Self-awareness — know responses, needs, limits
  6. General well-being — balance of study, rest, personal life
Concentration = directing attention to a task or experience and holding it for a suitable period. Calmness and concentration support each other: overwhelmed → hard to focus; constantly interrupted → frustrated and tired.
  1. Learning efficiency — engage with lessons
  2. Memory — attending when information is given
  3. Completing tasks — sustained effort
  4. Fewer mistakes — notice details, follow instructions
  5. Discipline — routines for directing attention
  6. Problem-solving — examine information, consider approaches
How they connect (related, not identical): Relaxation reduces needless physical and mental tension · Mental calmness = emotional steadiness, less reactive · Concentration points attention at a task or object · Meditation trains awareness and attention systematically.
Example: Shavasana to release tension → breath awareness to settle attention → concentrate on a textbook chapter.

Everyday use: before studying — 2 min comfortable breathing · before an exam — watch the breath briefly, unforced · long study session — pause, notice and relax tense shoulders · in class — return attention whenever the mind wanders · before reacting to something upsetting — recognise the emotion, choose a thoughtful reply · end of day — brief body scan for tension.

Supporting habits: regular sleep · balanced meals and enough water · regular physical activity · reasonably organised study space · breaks in long sessions · fewer digital distractions · healthy communication · relaxation/meditation regularly, without expecting instant results.

UNIT VIII

Basic Yoga Philosophy & Lifestyle

Yoga is an ancient Indian system of knowledge and practice for harmony of body, mind and overall well-being (Yuj = to join / unite / yoke). It balances physical activity, breathing, thoughts, emotions and behaviour. It is not just postures on a mat — it includes ethics, self-discipline, breath regulation, concentration, meditation and self-awareness; applied in daily life it becomes a way of living: balance, responsibility, kindness, healthy habits. A key base is Ashtanga Yoga from Patanjali's Yoga Sutras.

Learning objectives: explain meaning and concept of yoga · describe the 8 limbs · know Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama · explain Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi at intro level · see yoga as a way of life, not just exercise · value discipline and self-awareness · role of a balanced lifestyle · basics of a balanced yogic diet and healthy habits.

1. Concept of Ashtanga Yoga

Ashta = eight, Anga = limb / part → “Eight-Limbed Path”, from the Yoga Sutras (traditionally Patanjali): a framework for ethical behaviour, physical stability, breath awareness, control of attention and meditative awareness. Do not confuse it with the modern physical style Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga.

Importance: a broad framework for personal growth. Yama + Niyama = conduct and discipline; Asana + Pranayama = posture and breath; Pratyahara + Dharana + Dhyana = awareness and attention; Samadhi = deep absorption. The limbs are interconnected (e.g. physical practice helps you sit for meditation; ethics and discipline support a balanced life) — not a list of separate exercises but a complete approach to living and self-development.

Modern yoga: standing, sitting, balancing, twisting, lying postures. Not a competition — the aim is a comfortable, attentive, balanced relationship with the body.

5. Pranayama — Regulation of Breathing

Pranayama = awareness and regulation of breathing (prana = vital life force, ayama = extension / regulation). At beginner level it is gentle breath awareness and suitable breathing exercises. It is not simply taking very deep breaths; traditional techniques use different patterns and sometimes retention. Beginners first learn to breathe comfortably and safely.

Basic types: ① natural breath awareness ② gentle slow breathing ③ abdominal breathing ④ Nadi Shodhana (alternate nostril; learn from a qualified instructor, no forcing or holding) ⑤ Bhramari (humming bee; no voice or breath strain).

Simple gentle breathing (1–3 min): sit comfortably, spine naturally upright → relax shoulders, face, abdomen → breathe in gently → let the breath leave smoothly, no pushing → notice rhythm and sensation → return to normal breathing and notice how you feel.

Benefits: breathing awareness · may support relaxation and stress management · steady attention · notice breath changes with emotions · foundation for structured practices · complements asana and meditation. Precautions: no forceful breathing or long retention unless trained and medically suitable · stop for dizziness, breathlessness, discomfort · respiratory/cardiovascular conditions → professional advice · never a competition. Daily life: breathing changes when nervous before exams, excited in sports, frustrated by a hard task — awareness gives a chance to pause and respond thoughtfully.

6. Pratyahara — Withdrawal of the Senses

5th limb. Withdrawing or regulating attention from external sensory distractions. Senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch) are normal; constant attention to outside stimuli makes it hard to stay on a task. Pratyahara reduces automatic reactions to distractions and directs attention inward when appropriate. It does not mean avoiding the senses permanently or isolating yourself.

Simple exercise (2–5 min): sit comfortably, quiet place → notice sounds around you without chasing each → notice feet touching the floor → eyes closed or gaze lowered (if comfortable) → gradually move attention from outside distractions to natural breathing → a sound or sensation pulls you: notice it, return gently to your focus.

Daily life: phone away while studying · quiet place for reading or meditation · stay on task despite nearby distractions · break from digital notifications · sit quietly a few minutes instead of seeking stimulation. Benefits: awareness of sensory distractions · redirect attention to a chosen activity · supports concentration · more control over habitual reactions · a transition from outward activity toward meditation. Importance: a link between the earlier limbs and the later concentration and meditation practices — learning not to follow every distraction steadies attention.

7. Dharana — Concentration

6th limb. Fixing or directing attention on a chosen object, point, sound, thought or experience; takes intentional effort. When the mind wanders you notice and return to the chosen focus.

Examples: natural breath · a small dot on paper · repeating a word/mantra mentally · listening to a particular sound · full attention to a single study task.

Simple practice (2–5 min): sit comfortably, quiet place → choose one object (e.g. the breath) → direct attention to it → observe carefully → thoughts arise: recognise that attention wandered → return without criticising yourself.

Benefits: sustained attention · patience, mental discipline · learning and task completion · notice distractions · foundation for deeper meditation. Student life: reading a chapter and repeatedly returning to the text after distraction; listening to a teacher without checking the phone.

8. Dhyana — Meditation

7th limb. A sustained, relatively uninterrupted flow of attention toward the object of meditation. Dharana = directing and maintaining attention; Dhyana = attention becomes more continuous and less interrupted by unrelated thoughts. It does not need zero thoughts — it develops a stable, attentive relationship with the focus.

Basic practice (a few minutes): sit comfortably, spine naturally upright → relax the body → attention on natural breath or another suitable focus → observe without forcing a particular experience → thoughts arise: acknowledge, don't get absorbed → return gently to the focus → continue a few minutes → end slowly, back to surroundings.

Benefits: present-moment awareness · emotional regulation and stress management · patience and self-awareness · structured time for reflection · supports attention when regular · less reactive to thoughts and feelings. Daily life: a student quietly watching the breath and returning to it when distracted is learning the basic skills of meditation.

9. Samadhi — Basic Concept

8th limb. In classical philosophy, a state of deep meditative absorption in which awareness is profoundly absorbed in the object of meditation. An advanced concept — not ordinary relaxation, a temporary happy feeling, or absence of thoughts. For intro study, just know Samadhi = profound meditative absorption. Characteristics: deep absorption · highly focused, unified awareness · reduced distraction during the meditative experience · major place in traditional yoga philosophy.

Self-awareness = recognising your thoughts, emotions, habits, strengths, limitations and behaviour; grows through meditation, breath awareness, self-study and mindful observation.

13. Yogic Diet & Healthy Habits

Yogic diet = eating practices linked to traditional yogic principles of moderation, awareness and care for the body. Traditions differ; some stress Sattvic foods (simple, fresh, clear, moderate). No single diet suits everyone; yoga does not require the same food choices for all students. For intro study: a varied balanced diet, adequate hydration, moderation, a healthy relationship with food.

8 principles of a balanced diet: ① variety (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts/seeds, suitable protein foods) ② enough protein (pulses, beans, lentils, dairy, soy, eggs, fish — by preference) ③ nutritious carbohydrates (rice, roti, oats, millets) ④ vegetables and fruits of many colours (vitamins, minerals, fibre) ⑤ hydration — more in heat or exercise ⑥ moderation: limit added sugar, salt, unhealthy fats, with flexibility and enjoyment ⑦ food safety: wash hands, clean utensils, store properly, avoid spoiled food ⑧ eat by individual need — age, activity, health, culture, circumstances.

Eating habits encouraged in yoga: eat with awareness, don't rush · avoid routinely eating past comfortable fullness · prefer clean, safely prepared, properly stored food · regular meals when your schedule allows · don't turn food into guilt or needless restriction · nourishment matters for activity, learning and recovery. (Tradition: leave a little room in the stomach — modern lesson: eat comfortably and respond to hunger and fullness, no rigid rule for all.)

Why a balanced diet matters for students: energy through the day · growth and development · concentration and general cognitive function · recovery from activity · normal immune function · healthy habits for adulthood. Yoga must not be used to justify fasting, food deprivation or restrictive eating that may harm health.
