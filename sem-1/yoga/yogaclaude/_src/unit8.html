UNIT VIII

Basic Yoga Philosophy & Lifestyle

Yoga is an ancient Indian system of knowledge and practice for harmony of body, mind and overall well-being (Yuj = to join / unite / yoke). It balances physical activity, breathing, thoughts, emotions and behaviour. It is not just postures on a mat — it includes ethics, self-discipline, breath regulation, concentration, meditation and self-awareness; applied in daily life it becomes a way of living: balance, responsibility, kindness, healthy habits. A key base is Ashtanga Yoga from Patanjali's Yoga Sutras.

Learning objectives: explain meaning and concept of yoga · describe the 8 limbs · know Yama, Niyama, Asana, Pranayama · explain Pratyahara, Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi at intro level · see yoga as a way of life, not just exercise · value discipline and self-awareness · role of a balanced lifestyle · basics of a balanced yogic diet and healthy habits.

1. Concept of Ashtanga Yoga

Ashta = eight, Anga = limb / part → “Eight-Limbed Path”, from the Yoga Sutras (traditionally Patanjali): a framework for ethical behaviour, physical stability, breath awareness, control of attention and meditative awareness. Do not confuse it with the modern physical style Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga.

Ethical conduct — responsible, respectful dealings with othersPersonal discipline — cleanliness, contentment, self-discipline, self-studyPosture — stable, comfortable, attentiveRegulation of breathingWithdrawal of senses — less automatic attention to external distractionsConcentration on a chosen object or pointMeditation — sustained, continuous flow of attentionDeep meditative absorption

Importance: a broad framework for personal growth. Yama + Niyama = conduct and discipline; Asana + Pranayama = posture and breath; Pratyahara + Dharana + Dhyana = awareness and attention; Samadhi = deep absorption. The limbs are interconnected (e.g. physical practice helps you sit for meditation; ethics and discipline support a balanced life) — not a list of separate exercises but a complete approach to living and self-development.

2. Yama — Ethical Conduct (5)

Principles for how a person behaves toward people and the world: respect, honesty, responsibility, moderation, fairness — useful in family, class, friendships and society.

Does not mean accepting harm — you can set boundaries and seek help.
Satya

Brahmacharya
Moderation / responsible use of energy		Interpreted in different ways (disciplined conduct, regulating desires); basic student version: moderation, using time and energy responsiblyBalance study, recreation, rest · limit phone and social media · appropriate boundaries in relationships · time and energy for meaningful activities. Self-control without avoiding all enjoyment or social life.
Aparigraha
Non-possessiveness		Avoid excessive attachment, greed, hoarding; contentment and responsible use of resourcesNo unnecessary purchases · share resources · don't judge worth by possessions or status · be content without constant comparison. Happiness and worth are not decided only by possessions.

Importance: good character, respect, honesty, responsible use of resources → safer classrooms, healthier relationships, a more cooperative society.

4. Asana — Physical Posture

Asana = posture / body position, practised for stability, flexibility, balance, coordination and awareness. In the Yoga Sutras: a posture that is steady and comfortable. Modern yoga: standing, sitting, balancing, twisting, lying postures. Not a competition — the aim is a comfortable, attentive, balanced relationship with the body.

Basic asanaHowPurpose
Tadasana — MountainStand upright, feet comfortably placed, body naturally alignedPostural awareness, balance, stable standing
Vrikshasana — TreeBalance on one leg, other foot against a suitable part of the standing leg — no pressure on the kneeBalance, coordination, focused attention
Vajrasana — ThunderboltKneel, sit back toward the heels (if knees and ankles are comfortable)Upright sitting, body awareness
Bhujangasana — CobraLie on abdomen, gently raise the chest with back muscles; hands support as neededSpinal movement, posture awareness
Shavasana — CorpseLie on back, consciously relax while staying awareRelaxation, recovery after activity
Benefits: flexibility and mobility · balance and coordination · posture and alignment awareness · strength and fitness (depends on postures) · awareness of sensations and movement · chance to release tension · patience and consistency.
Guidelines: non-slip surface · comfortable clothes · simple postures first, progress gradually · no jerks or forced stretching · breathe naturally unless told otherwise · pain, dizziness or unusual discomfort → stop or modify · injury or health condition → seek guidance.

Part of philosophy: asana is one of the 8 limbs, but yoga as a whole goes beyond postures — ethics, discipline, breathing, concentration and meditation are also essential.

5. Pranayama — Regulation of Breathing

Pranayama = awareness and regulation of breathing (prana = vital life force, ayama = extension / regulation). At beginner level it is gentle breath awareness and suitable breathing exercises. It is not simply taking very deep breaths; traditional techniques use different patterns and sometimes retention. Beginners first learn to breathe comfortably and safely.

Basic types: ① natural breath awareness ② gentle slow breathing ③ abdominal breathing ④ Nadi Shodhana (alternate nostril; learn from a qualified instructor, no forcing or holding) ⑤ Bhramari (humming bee; no voice or breath strain).

Simple gentle breathing (1–3 min): sit comfortably, spine naturally upright → relax shoulders, face, abdomen → breathe in gently → let the breath leave smoothly, no pushing → notice rhythm and sensation → return to normal breathing and notice how you feel.

Benefits: breathing awareness · may support relaxation and stress management · steady attention · notice breath changes with emotions · foundation for structured practices · complements asana and meditation. Precautions: no forceful breathing or long retention unless trained and medically suitable · stop for dizziness, breathlessness, discomfort · respiratory/cardiovascular conditions → professional advice · never a competition. Daily life: breathing changes when nervous before exams, excited in sports, frustrated by a hard task — awareness gives a chance to pause and respond thoughtfully.

6. Pratyahara — Withdrawal of the Senses

5th limb. Withdrawing or regulating attention from external sensory distractions. Senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch) are normal; constant attention to outside stimuli makes it hard to stay on a task. Pratyahara reduces automatic reactions to distractions and directs attention inward when appropriate. It does not mean avoiding the senses permanently or isolating yourself.

Simple exercise (2–5 min): sit comfortably, quiet place → notice sounds around you without chasing each → notice feet touching the floor → eyes closed or gaze lowered (if comfortable) → gradually move attention from outside distractions to natural breathing → a sound or sensation pulls you: notice it, return gently to your focus.

Daily life: phone away while studying · quiet place for reading or meditation · stay on task despite nearby distractions · break from digital notifications · sit quietly a few minutes instead of seeking stimulation. Benefits: awareness of sensory distractions · redirect attention to a chosen activity · supports concentration · more control over habitual reactions · a transition from outward activity toward meditation. Importance: a link between the earlier limbs and the later concentration and meditation practices — learning not to follow every distraction steadies attention.

7. Dharana — Concentration

6th limb. Fixing or directing attention on a chosen object, point, sound, thought or experience; takes intentional effort. When the mind wanders you notice and return to the chosen focus.

Examples: natural breath · a small dot on paper · repeating a word/mantra mentally · listening to a particular sound · full attention to a single study task.

Simple practice (2–5 min): sit comfortably, quiet place → choose one object (e.g. the breath) → direct attention to it → observe carefully → thoughts arise: recognise that attention wandered → return without criticising yourself.

Benefits: sustained attention · patience, mental discipline · learning and task completion · notice distractions · foundation for deeper meditation. Student life: reading a chapter and repeatedly returning to the text after distraction; listening to a teacher without checking the phone.

8. Dhyana — Meditation

7th limb. A sustained, relatively uninterrupted flow of attention toward the object of meditation. Dharana = directing and maintaining attention; Dhyana = attention becomes more continuous and less interrupted by unrelated thoughts. It does not need zero thoughts — it develops a stable, attentive relationship with the focus.

Basic practice (a few minutes): sit comfortably, spine naturally upright → relax the body → attention on natural breath or another suitable focus → observe without forcing a particular experience → thoughts arise: acknowledge, don't get absorbed → return gently to the focus → continue a few minutes → end slowly, back to surroundings.

Benefits: present-moment awareness · emotional regulation and stress management · patience and self-awareness · structured time for reflection · supports attention when regular · less reactive to thoughts and feelings. Daily life: a student quietly watching the breath and returning to it when distracted is learning the basic skills of meditation.

9. Samadhi — Basic Concept

8th limb. In classical philosophy, a state of deep meditative absorption in which awareness is profoundly absorbed in the object of meditation. An advanced concept — not ordinary relaxation, a temporary happy feeling, or absence of thoughts. For intro study, just know Samadhi = profound meditative absorption. Characteristics: deep absorption · highly focused, unified awareness · reduced distraction during the meditative experience · major place in traditional yoga philosophy.

LimbBasic meaningSimple explanation
DharanaConcentrationDirecting attention to a chosen focus
DhyanaMeditationSustaining a continuous flow of attention
SamadhiMeditative absorptionDeep absorption in the object of meditation

Progressively refined aspects of attention — not a rigid sequence everyone experiences the same way. Importance: shows the direction of the eight-limbed path — yoga cares not only for physical health but for attention, awareness and deeper meditative understanding. Students are not expected to attain Samadhi in a basic course.

10. Yoga as a Way of Life

Applying yogic principles beyond formal sessions — in daily activities, relationships, study, work, eating, resting, decisions. 20 minutes of postures = a physical practice; applying honesty, moderation, patience and self-awareness all day = yoga extended into life.

8 aspects: ① physical well-being (activity, mobility, posture, rest) ② mental well-being (awareness of thoughts, manage distractions, make time for relaxation) ③ ethical behaviour ④ emotional balance (respond, don't react impulsively) ⑤ self-discipline ⑥ healthy relationships (respectful communication, careful listening, boundaries) ⑦ environmental responsibility (clean surroundings, less waste, responsible use of resources) ⑧ self-awareness (habits, strengths, difficulties, improvements).

11. Discipline & Self-Awareness

Discipline = following suitable rules, routines and commitments even when it needs effort; helps organise activities, manage time, work toward goals. In yoga it connects with Tapas, regular practice and responsible habits. Not harshness to yourself: realistic goals, consistency, flexibility when circumstances change, learning from mistakes.
  1. Consistency — regular practice improves skills
  2. Time management — balance study, rest, recreation
  3. Responsibility — finish tasks, keep commitments
  4. Perseverance — keep going when progress is slow
  5. Healthy habits — regular sleep, activity, meals
Examples: on time for classes · realistic timetable · safe, regular yoga · assignments before deadlines · breaks instead of studying to exhaustion · follow safety rules in class.
Self-awareness = recognising your thoughts, emotions, habits, strengths, limitations and behaviour; grows through meditation, breath awareness, self-study and mindful observation.
  1. Physical — posture, movement, tiredness, tension
  2. Emotional — anger, fear, sadness, excitement, happiness
  3. Mental — thinking, attention, distraction patterns
  4. Behavioural — habits and their effect on others
  5. Social — others' views, feelings, boundaries
How to build it: observe the breath a few minutes · notice tension while studying · name emotions before reacting · reflect on daily successes and mistakes · ask for constructive feedback · know strengths and weak areas · set realistic goals from the reflection.

Example: irritated at an unsolved problem → recognise the frustration, pause, a few comfortable breaths, return with a clearer plan. Frustration may not vanish, but you respond more thoughtfully. Together: discipline keeps useful habits going; self-awareness tells you which habits help and which need change (a timetable = discipline; reflecting on concentration, tiredness and progress = self-awareness).

Organising daily life to support physical health, mental well-being, learning, relationships, recreation and rest — not equal time for everything every day, but meeting different needs with reasonable adjustments.

13. Yogic Diet & Healthy Habits

Yogic diet = eating practices linked to traditional yogic principles of moderation, awareness and care for the body. Traditions differ; some stress Sattvic foods (simple, fresh, clear, moderate). No single diet suits everyone; yoga does not require the same food choices for all students. For intro study: a varied balanced diet, adequate hydration, moderation, a healthy relationship with food.

GunaQualityTraditional food idea
SattvaClarity, balance, harmonySattvic food: fresh, simple, moderate, clean, balanced — fresh fruits, vegetables, whole grains, pulses, nuts, suitable dairy
RajasActivity, stimulation, restlessnessRajasic food: stimulating — very spicy, strongly flavoured foods in some descriptions
TamasInertia, heaviness, inactivityTamasic food: heavy / dulling — stale, spoiled, some heavily processed foods (interpretations differ)

The Gunas are philosophical / traditional classifications, not scientific nutrition categories, and not a universal medical judgement. Practical guide: avoid spoiled or unsafe food, limit foods that crowd out nutritious ones; freshness, food safety and overall diet quality matter more than the labels.

8 principles of a balanced diet: ① variety (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts/seeds, suitable protein foods) ② enough protein (pulses, beans, lentils, dairy, soy, eggs, fish — by preference) ③ nutritious carbohydrates (rice, roti, oats, millets) ④ vegetables and fruits of many colours (vitamins, minerals, fibre) ⑤ hydration — more in heat or exercise ⑥ moderation: limit added sugar, salt, unhealthy fats, with flexibility and enjoyment ⑦ food safety: wash hands, clean utensils, store properly, avoid spoiled food ⑧ eat by individual need — age, activity, health, culture, circumstances.

Eating habits encouraged in yoga: eat with awareness, don't rush · avoid routinely eating past comfortable fullness · prefer clean, safely prepared, properly stored food · regular meals when your schedule allows · don't turn food into guilt or needless restriction · nourishment matters for activity, learning and recovery. (Tradition: leave a little room in the stomach — modern lesson: eat comfortably and respond to hunger and fullness, no rigid rule for all.)

Why a balanced diet matters for students: energy through the day · growth and development · concentration and general cognitive function · recovery from activity · normal immune function · healthy habits for adulthood. Yoga must not be used to justify fasting, food deprivation or restrictive eating that may harm health.

Healthy habits beyond diet
Personal hygiene — bathe, wash hands, oral hygiene, clean clothesRegular movement — avoid long sitting; suitable physical activity
Adequate rest — sleep routine, recovery after demanding activityMental hygiene — relaxation, enjoyable activities, healthy social connections
Responsible technology use — limit distractions; don't let devices keep disrupting sleep, study or relationshipsEnvironmental cleanliness — clean living and study spaces, dispose of waste responsibly
Moderation — avoid excessive or harmful habits; seek support when needed
Individual needs: don't start restrictive diets, cut out whole food groups or use fasting as “yogic discipline” without guidance. Vegetarian and non-vegetarian diets can both meet nutritional needs. Medical conditions, food allergies or special requirements → follow a qualified healthcare professional or registered dietitian.
